# Global Internal Audit Standards (IIA, 2024) and the Cybersecurity Topical Requirement

## At a glance

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Publisher / standard setter | The Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA). Set by the International Internal Audit Standards Board (IIASB) under due process overseen by the IPPF Oversight Council |
| Instrument | Global Internal Audit Standards ("the Standards", published as *The Redbook*) — a mandatory component of the International Professional Practices Framework (IPPF) |
| Published / effective | Published **9 January 2024**; effective **9 January 2025** |
| Structure | **5 domains → 15 principles → 52 standards.** Domains II–V carry the principles and standards; Domain I states the Purpose of Internal Auditing |
| Anatomy of a standard | *Requirements* ("must"), *Considerations for Implementation* ("should"/"may"), *Examples of Evidence of Conformance*. Domain III standards add **Essential Conditions** — what the board and senior management must do |
| Second mandatory layer | **Topical Requirements** — mandatory for assurance services, recommended for advisory services; each becomes effective 12 months after issue |
| Cybersecurity Topical Requirement | Issued **5 February 2025**, effective **5 February 2026**. Three requirement areas — Governance (A–D), Risk Management (A–F), Controls (A–G): 17 requirements in total |
| Who is covered | All internal audit functions are expected to conform. Not a control framework for the enterprise — it governs the *audit function* and the way it assesses other people's controls |
| Certifiable? | No certification. Conformance is demonstrated through a quality assurance and improvement program: internal assessments plus an **external quality assessment at least once every five years** by a qualified independent assessor (or self-assessment with independent validation), with at least one assessor holding an active Certified Internal Auditor designation |
| Enforcement | Professional, not statutory. Nonconformance must be documented and disclosed to the board and senior management; conformance is evaluated during quality assessments. Individual jurisdictions, regulators and public-sector mandates may make the Standards legally binding — check locally (verify) |
| Recommended layer | Global Guidance — Global Practice Guides and Global Technology Audit Guides (GTAGs) — is recommended, not mandatory |

## What it is

The Global Internal Audit Standards are the profession's rulebook for how an internal audit function is authorized, positioned, managed and performed. The 2024 edition is the product of the IIASB's "IPPF Evolution" project, which ran from 2021 through December 2023 and restructured the framework: the **Code of Ethics, Core Principles and Definition of Internal Auditing were folded into the Standards** rather than remaining separate IPPF elements, and recommended implementation guidance was appended directly to each requirement instead of living in standalone Implementation Guides. The result replaces the 2017 International Standards for the Professional Practice of Internal Auditing and their satellite documents with a single mandatory text.

Two things make the 2024 edition matter to security and GRC teams rather than only to auditors. First, **Domain III makes the board an addressee.** Its "essential conditions" spell out what the board and senior management must do — authorize the mandate, approve the charter, appoint and remove the chief audit executive, approve the plan and resources, receive external quality assessment results directly from the assessor. Where a board does not provide a condition, the chief audit executive must document the reason and any compensating alternative.

Second, the 2024 IPPF added an entirely new mandatory content type: **Topical Requirements**, which set a minimum baseline for *how a specific risk topic must be audited*. Cybersecurity was the first one issued. A Topical Requirement does not oblige a function to audit cybersecurity; it obliges the function to cover a defined baseline whenever it does. This is the first time the profession has fixed a mandatory floor for the content of a cyber audit, and it is the point at which the Standards intersect directly with the security programme.

## Who it covers / Scope

- **Every internal audit function**, in any sector, size or country, is expected to conform; the Standards explicitly acknowledge small functions and the public sector as contexts that may need documented alternative actions rather than exemptions.
- **Conformance is claimable only in full.** Where law or regulation prevents conformance with part of the Standards, conformance with all other parts is still required and the departure must be disclosed. Where the Standards are used alongside another authoritative body's requirements, internal audit communications must cite that other source as well.
- **Topical Requirement trigger (Cybersecurity):** the requirement applies when the topic is (A) the subject of an engagement in the internal audit plan, (B) identified while performing an engagement, or (C) the subject of an engagement request not on the original plan. Applicability of each individual requirement must be assessed, documented and retained; excluded requirements need a documented rationale.
- **Not a scope test for the enterprise.** Nothing here tells you whether a control is required; it tells you what internal audit must do before it can conclude on one.

## Structure and requirements

| Domain | Principles | Standards | Substance |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Purpose of Internal Auditing | — | — | The value proposition internal audit exists to deliver |
| II — Ethics and Professionalism | 1–5 (13 standards) | 1.1–5.2 | Integrity, objectivity, competency, due professional care, confidentiality; includes 4.1 Conformance with the Standards |
| III — Governing the Internal Audit Function | 6–8 (9 standards) | 6.1–8.4 | Mandate, charter, board/senior-management support, organizational independence, CAE qualifications, board interaction, resources, quality, external quality assessment |
| IV — Managing the Internal Audit Function | 9–12 (16 standards) | 9.1–12.3 | Strategy, methodologies, the audit plan, coordination and reliance, financial/human/technological resources, communication, internal quality assessment, performance measurement |
| V — Performing Internal Audit Services | 13–15 (14 standards) | 13.1–15.2 | Engagement planning, risk assessment, objectives and scope, evaluation criteria, work programs, fieldwork, findings, recommendations, conclusions, documentation, reporting, follow-up |

Standards that most often bind a security or GRC counterparty:

| Standard | Requirement (abridged) |
|---|---|
| 4.1 Conformance with the Global Internal Audit Standards | Methodologies must be established, documented and maintained in alignment with the Standards; nonconformance must be documented and communicated with the circumstance, alternative actions taken, impact and rationale |
| 7.1 Organizational Independence | The CAE must confirm organizational independence to the board **at least annually**, including any impairments and safeguards. Ongoing non-audit roles must be documented in the charter with their safeguards, and where those areas are themselves audited, assurance must come from an alternative process such as an objective external provider reporting to the board; for temporary non-audit roles, an independent third party must provide assurance during the assignment **and for the subsequent 12 months** |
| 8.4 External Quality Assessment | External assessment **at least once every five years** by a qualified, independent assessor or team (or self-assessment with independent validation); at least one assessor must hold an active CIA. The board receives the complete results directly from the assessor |
| 9.4 Internal Audit Plan | A documented, organizationwide risk assessment **at least annually** underpins the plan; the plan must consider coverage of IT governance, fraud risk and the effectiveness of compliance and ethics programmes; board approval of the plan is an essential condition under Standard 6.3 |
| 9.5 Coordination and Reliance | The CAE must coordinate with internal and external assurance providers and consider relying on their work; the basis for reliance must be documented, and internal audit remains responsible for its own conclusions |
| 10.3 Technological Resources | The CAE must collaborate with the IT and information security functions when implementing audit technology and must report technology limitations to the board |
| 11.5 Communicating the Acceptance of Risks | Where management has accepted risk beyond appetite or tolerance, the CAE must discuss it with senior management and escalate unresolved matters to the board — but is not responsible for resolving the risk |
| 12.1 Internal Quality Assessment | Ongoing monitoring plus periodic self-assessments; results and action plans communicated to the board; material nonconformance affecting the function's scope or operation must be disclosed |
| 14.6 Engagement Documentation | Documentation sufficient for an informed, prudent internal auditor to repeat the work and reach the same results; reviewed by the supervisor and approved by the CAE |

### The Cybersecurity Topical Requirement (effective 5 February 2026)

Seventeen mandatory assessment points across three areas. Internal auditors *must assess* each of the following about the organization:

| Area | Requirements |
|---|---|
| **Governance** (A–D) | A: a formal cybersecurity strategy and objectives, periodically updated, with progress, resources and budget reviewed by the board. B: cybersecurity policies and procedures established and periodically updated. C: roles and responsibilities defined, with a process to periodically assess the knowledge, skills and abilities of the people in them. D: relevant stakeholders — senior management, operations, risk, HR, legal, compliance, vendors — engaged on existing vulnerabilities and emerging threats |
| **Risk management** (A–F) | A: cyber threats identified, analyzed, mitigated and monitored within enterprise risk processes, linked to strategic objectives. B: cyber risk management conducted across the organization (IT, ERM, HR, legal, compliance, operations, supply chain, finance). C: named accountability with periodic monitoring and reporting. D: a rapid escalation process when a cyber risk exceeds acceptable levels under risk guidelines or legal/regulatory requirements, considering financial and nonfinancial impact. E: risk awareness communicated, with management review and timely remediation of gaps, deficiencies and control failures. F: an incident response and recovery process covering detection, containment, recovery and post-incident analysis, **periodically tested** |
| **Controls** (A–G) | A: internal and vendor-based controls protecting confidentiality, integrity and availability, evaluated periodically. B: talent management and technical cybersecurity training, periodically reviewed. C: continuous monitoring and reporting of emerging threats and vulnerabilities. D: cybersecurity embedded in IT asset lifecycle management (selection, use, maintenance, decommissioning), including vendor services. E: configuration, endpoint administration, encryption, patching, user-access management, availability/performance monitoring, and security in software development. F: network controls — access control and segmentation, firewalls, limits on external connections, VPN/ZTNA, IoT controls, IDS/IPS. G: endpoint-communication security for email, browsers, videoconferencing, messaging, social media, cloud and file sharing |

The companion **User Guide** maps each requirement to NIST CSF 2.0, NIST SP 800-53 and COBIT 2019 — for example, Governance A maps to CSF GV.RM-01 to GV.RM-04, GV.OC-02, GV.RR-03/04, GV.PO-01/02 and GV.OV-01/03, and to 800-53 PM-1, PM-4, PM-9, PM-28, AT-2 and AT-3. Use that mapping to reconcile an existing cyber audit programme to the baseline rather than rebuilding it. See [nist-csf-2.md](nist-csf-2.md) and [nist-800-53.md](nist-800-53.md).

## Assessment, certification and evidence

- **No certificate exists.** The deliverable is a conformance opinion from a quality assessment, not a certification mark. Treat "conforms with the Global Internal Audit Standards" as a claim that must be supported by the QAIP record.
- **Two layers.** Internal: ongoing monitoring embedded in supervision plus periodic self-assessments, with action plans and board communication (Standard 12.1). External: an EQA or a self-assessment with independent validation at least every five years (Standard 8.4). Internal assessments must be documented and are reviewed by the external assessor.
- **Trigger a more frequent EQA** on a change of CAE or senior management, a merger of audit functions, significant methodology change, or significant staff turnover; regulated industries often shorten the cycle by requirement.
- **Application Guidance sets a "conform or explain" expectation.** The Topical Requirements Application Guidance (issued 29 August 2025) explains applying the requirements with professional judgment and a risk-based approach, and requires documentation and communication where a function deviates because of resource or regulatory constraints.
- **Conformance with Topical Requirements is evaluated during quality assessments.** The evidence expected is documentation that each requirement was assessed for applicability, plus a documented rationale for any excluded requirement — keep this in the engagement file, not in a separate memo.
- **Evidence of conformance** is enumerated per standard in the text itself (methodology documents, board minutes, charters, workpapers, QAIP results). Use [templates/control-test-workpaper.md](../../templates/control-test-workpaper.md) and [templates/audit-evidence-request-list.md](../../templates/audit-evidence-request-list.md) to structure engagement files to that bar.

As of September 2026 the Standards remain the 2024 edition — no amendment or new edition has been issued, and four Topical Requirements have been issued of which two (Cybersecurity, Third-Party) are in force. The IIASB's public-interest framework commits it to reviewing the IPPF's elements at least once every three years, so expect maintenance changes rather than a further restructure in the near term. The Anti-Corruption Topical Requirement (due Q4 2026) and Talent Management (public consultation January 2027) are still pending.

## Key obligations for security/GRC teams

1. **Expect the cyber audit to follow a fixed baseline.** From 5 February 2026, an assurance engagement on cybersecurity must cover all 17 requirement points unless an exclusion is documented. Pre-map your control set to them before scoping starts — see [audit-preparation](../../skills/audit-preparation/SKILL.md) and [workflows/audit-readiness.md](../../workflows/audit-readiness.md).
2. **Produce the governance artefacts the requirement names**: a dated cybersecurity strategy with objectives, board-level reporting on progress and budget, a current policy set, a role/responsibility matrix, and a documented competency assessment for those roles. See [policy-authoring](../../skills/policy-authoring/SKILL.md).
3. **Show a tested incident response and recovery process** with detection, containment, recovery and post-incident analysis, plus evidence of periodic testing — Risk Management requirement F is where most functions fail first. See [control-testing](../../skills/control-testing/SKILL.md).
4. **Wire cyber risk into enterprise risk management**, including a rapid escalation path for risks that breach appetite or a legal/regulatory threshold. Standard 11.5 obliges the CAE to escalate accepted-but-excessive risk to the board, so your exception register and the audit file will be read side by side — see [exception-management](../../skills/exception-management/SKILL.md) and [risk-assessment](../../skills/risk-assessment/SKILL.md).
5. **Treat third-party cyber controls as in scope twice over** — Controls requirement A covers vendor-based controls, and the Third-Party Topical Requirement has been in force since 15 September 2026. See [third-party-risk-assessment](../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md).
6. **Offer your work for reliance, deliberately.** Standard 9.5 requires the CAE to coordinate with other assurance providers and document the basis for reliance. Second-line testing that is documented to a reperformable standard reduces duplicate fieldwork; testing that is not will simply be redone.
7. **Support the annual plan with real risk data.** The plan rests on a documented organizationwide risk assessment refreshed at least annually and must consider IT governance and fraud risk — feed it your threat and control-failure data rather than letting audit infer it. See [grc-metrics-reporting](../../skills/grc-metrics-reporting/SKILL.md).
8. **Use the User Guide's framework mapping in both directions** to demonstrate coverage without maintaining a parallel control library. See [control-mapping](../../skills/control-mapping/SKILL.md) and [framework-gap-assessment](../../skills/framework-gap-assessment/SKILL.md).
9. **Know the quality cycle.** An EQA at least every five years will test whether the function's cyber engagements conformed to the Topical Requirement — engagement documentation from 2026 onward is the evidence base. See [agents/internal-auditor.md](../../agents/internal-auditor.md).

## Interplay

- **NIST CSF 2.0 / 800-53 / COBIT 2019:** the mapping layer, not a substitute — the Topical Requirement is a requirement on the *auditor*, while CSF and 800-53 are requirements on the *organization*. See [nist-csf-2.md](nist-csf-2.md), [nist-800-53.md](nist-800-53.md) and the [framework crosswalk](../crosswalks/framework-crosswalk.md).
- **ISO/IEC 27001:** the ISMS internal audit requirement and the Standards can be satisfied by the same function, but an ISO internal audit is scoped to the ISMS, while an IIA-conformant cyber engagement must cover the 17 baseline points. See [iso-27001-2022.md](iso-27001-2022.md).
- **SOX ITGC:** external auditors' ability to rely on internal audit work turns on competence and objectivity assessed under their own auditing standards, not on IIA conformance — but Standards 7.1 (independence confirmed annually) and 14.6 (reperformable documentation) are the practical enablers of that reliance. See [sox-itgc.md](../regulations/sox-itgc.md).
- **SOC 2:** internal audit is not the service auditor; its testing can inform readiness but cannot substitute for the CPA firm's procedures. See [soc2-tsc.md](soc2-tsc.md).
- **DORA, NIS2 and SEC cyber disclosure:** each imposes board-level cyber oversight duties that internal audit is often asked to assure. The Cybersecurity Topical Requirement's Governance area is a usable test programme for those duties. See [dora.md](../regulations/dora.md), [nis2.md](../regulations/nis2.md), [sec-cyber-disclosure.md](../regulations/sec-cyber-disclosure.md).
- **Three Lines Model:** the July 2026 Statement of Position positions internal audit as the integrator of assurance and is explicit that outsourcing does not change accountability — useful when arguing that a vendor's own attestation is not the end of the assurance chain.
- **CIS Controls:** a practical source of test procedures for the Controls area (configuration, patching, access, network, endpoint) when the organization has no formal control framework. See [cis-controls-v8.md](cis-controls-v8.md).
