# Breach & Incident Notification Timelines

Deadline matrix across major regimes. Use this to build notification decision trees and incident-response playbooks; then verify each applicable regime against official text and current guidance before filing anything. Clock-start definitions differ materially between regimes — see the nuance section, it is where most real-world failures happen.

Scope note: "breach" below covers both personal-data breaches (GDPR, HIPAA, state laws) and operational/security incidents (NIS2, DORA, SEC, banking rule), plus the neighbouring clocks that run on the same event — ransom and extortion-payment reports, product vulnerability reports, and contractual notices up and down the supply chain. A single event frequently triggers several regimes at once with different clocks.

How to use it: run the applicability check first ([../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md)), pull only the rows that apply, then open the linked context pack for each one — the pack, not this row, is the authority for thresholds, exemptions and content requirements. Log every clock you identify, with its start moment, in [../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md](../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md).

The matrix is split by region: [European Union](#european-union) · [United Kingdom](#united-kingdom) · [United States — federal](#united-states--federal) · [United States — state](#united-states--state) · [Asia-Pacific](#asia-pacific) · [Americas, Middle East & Africa](#americas-middle-east--africa) · [Sector and contractual regimes](#sector-and-contractual-regimes).

## European Union

EU instruments stack rather than displace each other: one event can be a GDPR breach, a NIS2 or DORA incident and a product-security report simultaneously. Clocks are calendar time unless the instrument says otherwise. Switzerland is listed here for proximity, not because it is in scope of EU law.

| Regime | Who must notify | Trigger + clock start | Deadline | Recipient | Content pointer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [GDPR Art. 33](../regulations/gdpr.md) | Controller (processor notifies controller) | Personal data breach; clock starts on controller *awareness*; exempt if unlikely to result in risk to individuals | 72 hours (calendar); phased notification permitted with reasons for delay | Competent supervisory authority (lead SA if cross-border) | Art. 33(3): nature, categories & approx. numbers of subjects/records, DPO contact, likely consequences, measures taken/proposed |
| [GDPR Art. 34](../regulations/gdpr.md) | Controller | Breach likely to result in *high risk* to individuals | Without undue delay | Affected data subjects | Plain-language description; same core elements as Art. 33(3); exemptions incl. effective encryption or subsequent risk-eliminating measures |
| [NIS2 Art. 23](../regulations/nis2.md) | Essential & important entities | *Significant* incident; clock starts on awareness | 24 h early warning → 72 h incident notification → intermediate on request → final report within 1 month | CSIRT or competent authority; service recipients where relevant | Early warning: suspected unlawful/malicious cause, cross-border impact. 72 h: initial assessment, severity, impact, IoCs. Final: root cause, mitigation, cross-border impact |
| [NIS2 national transpositions](../regulations/eu-nis2-implementing-and-transposition.md) | Entities as classified by each Member State's transposing law | Same significance test; Implementing Regulation 2024/2690 Arts. 3–14 quantify it for the digital sectors it covers | National law carries the Art. 23 clocks — Germany's BSIG § 32 is the pattern: early warning ≤ 24 h, full notification ≤ 72 h, interim on request, final ≤ 1 month after the 72-h notification ([pack](../frameworks/germany-bsi-it-grundschutz-c5.md)) | National CSIRT / competent authority via the national portal (Germany: a joint BSI/BBK reporting point) | Portals and registration routes differ per state; registration is a separate clock (Germany: register with BSI within 3 months of first qualifying) |
| [EU DORA Art. 19](../regulations/dora.md) + [RTS/ITS](../regulations/eu-dora-technical-standards.md) | Financial entities | *Major* ICT-related incident; clock starts on classification as major, against the RTS classification criteria | Initial notification as early as possible, within 4 h of classifying the incident as major and no later than 24 h from becoming aware (if classified major after the 24-h mark, within 4 h of classification); intermediate within 72 h of the initial notification even if nothing changed; final no later than 1 month after the intermediate report (Reg. 2025/301 Art. 5) | Competent financial authority, via its secure electronic channel | One template for all three stages (Reg. 2025/302 Annex I); voluntary cyber-threat notifications use Annexes III/IV; reclassification from major to non-major must be notified |
| [CER Directive Art. 15](../regulations/eu-cer-directive.md) | Critical entities identified by a Member State | Incident that significantly disrupts, or could significantly disrupt, provision of an essential service; clock starts on awareness | Initial notification without undue delay and no later than 24 h after becoming aware, unless operationally unable; detailed report where relevant no later than 1 month after it | Competent authority (which informs other affected Member States and, where 6+ are affected, the Commission) | Nature, cause, possible consequences, and what is needed to judge cross-border impact; significance parameters are users affected, duration and geography |
| [Cyber Resilience Act Art. 14](../regulations/eu-cyber-resilience-act.md) | Manufacturers of products with digital elements (open-source stewards to the extent involved) | Actively exploited vulnerability, or severe incident affecting product security | Early warning 24 h → notification 72 h → final report 14 days after a fix is available (vulnerabilities) or 1 month after the 72-h notification (incidents); reporting applies since 11 September 2026 | Simultaneously the CSIRT designated as coordinator and ENISA, through the Single Reporting Platform (Art. 16) | Early warning: whether unlawful or malicious acts are suspected, Member States affected; the receiving CSIRT may request intermediate reports |
| [eIDAS2 Arts. 19a, 24](../regulations/eu-eidas2.md) | Trust service providers and wallet providers | Breach or disruption with significant impact on the service or on the personal data held; clock starts on becoming aware | Without undue delay and no later than 24 hours | Supervisory body; identifiable affected individuals; the public where of public interest; other competent authorities | Certificate revocation on request is a separate 24-hour clock (Art. 24(3)) |
| [EHDS Art. 44(7)](../regulations/eu-ehds.md) | Manufacturers of EHR systems | Serious incident (death/serious harm, serious prejudice to rights, or serious disruption of health-sector critical infrastructure); report immediately after establishing a causal link or its reasonable likelihood | No later than 3 days after becoming aware; general application of the Regulation from 26 March 2027 | Market surveillance authorities of the Member States where the incident occurred and where the system is on the market | Corrective action taken or envisaged; expressly without prejudice to NIS2 notification |
| [ePrivacy / Reg. 611/2013](../regulations/eu-eprivacy.md) | Providers of publicly available electronic communications services | Personal data breach; clock starts on detection | No later than 24 hours after detection where feasible; second notification within 3 days of the initial one | Competent national authority | Reg. 611/2013 Art. 2 content set; sits alongside, not instead of, GDPR Arts. 33–34 |
| [EU AI Act Arts. 72–73](../regulations/eu-ai-act.md) | Providers of high-risk AI systems; systemic-risk GPAI providers also owe serious-incident reporting | Serious incident, once the provider establishes the causal link | Short statutory clocks tiered by severity — **verify the exact day-counts in Art. 73 before relying on them** | Market surveillance authorities | Post-market monitoring plan feeds the report; a serious incident involving personal data usually also runs the GDPR clocks |
| [Switzerland (non-EU)](../regulations/switzerland-fadp-isa.md) | Controllers (FADP); critical-infrastructure operators (ISA); FINMA-supervised institutions | FADP Art. 24: breach likely to result in *high risk*. ISA Art. 74e: cyberattack on critical infrastructure, clock from discovery | FDPIC "as quickly as possible"; NCSC/BACS within 24 hours of discovery with completion within 14 days (CSO Art. 16); FINMA preliminary notification within 24 hours, EHP report within 72 hours | FDPIC; NCSC/BACS; FINMA | FDPIC guidance: where high risk cannot quickly be excluded, notify — and notify *before* deciding on any ransom payment |

## United Kingdom

The UK runs the retained GDPR clock plus sector regimes; two major changes are pending — the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill and the 2027 FCA/PRA incident-reporting regime — so check in-force status before using the future rows.

| Regime | Who must notify | Trigger + clock start | Deadline | Recipient | Content pointer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [UK GDPR Arts. 33–34](../regulations/uk-data-protection.md) | Controller (processor notifies controller without undue delay) | Personal data breach; clock starts on awareness | 72 hours to the ICO; individuals without undue delay where high risk | ICO; affected individuals | Same content set as EU GDPR Art. 33(3) |
| [PECR reg. 5A](../regulations/uk-data-protection.md) | Providers of public electronic communications services | Personal data breach affecting the service | 72 hours since 20 August 2025 (previously 24 hours under retained Reg. 611/2013) | ICO | See also [../regulations/eu-eprivacy.md](../regulations/eu-eprivacy.md) for the EU-side clock |
| [NIS Regulations 2018 regs 11–12](../regulations/uk-nis-cyber-security-resilience.md) | OES (energy, transport, health, water, digital infrastructure); RDSPs | Incident with a significant (OES) or substantial (RDSP) impact on service continuity; clock starts on first awareness | No later than 72 hours after first awareness (OES notification is also "without undue delay") | Sector competent authority for OES; ICO for RDSPs | Operator name and services, time, duration, nature and impact, cross-border impact; RDSP thresholds from Art. 3 of Regulation 2018/151 plus ICO parameters (e.g. >750,000 user-hours unavailable) |
| [Cyber Security and Resilience Bill (not yet in force)](../regulations/uk-nis-cyber-security-resilience.md) | Expanded entity set, including data centres, RDSPs and managed service providers | Reportable incident; clock starts on awareness | Initial notification within 24 hours, fuller report within 72 hours | Regulator, with NCSC informed at the same time; customer notification duties for data centres, RDSPs and RMSPs | Track passage before wiring the 24-hour step into playbooks |
| [FCA/PRA operational incident reporting (from 18 March 2027)](../regulations/uk-financial-operational-resilience.md) | All firms with Part 4A permission, PSPs, UK RIEs, registered trade repositories and credit rating agencies; PRA-side for banks, designated investment firms and Solvency II firms | A reporting threshold is met — consumer harm, market integrity, or safety and soundness; clock starts on that determination | Initial report as soon as practicable, with a guidance expectation of at most 24 hours after determining the threshold is met; PSPs keep the 4-hours-from-detection rule; enhanced-reporting firms update at the intermediate phase and finalise within 30 working days of resolution (60 working days absolute) | One portal per regulator (FCA Connect for incidents and notifications) | Standard reporting is a single short report with no updates; enhanced reporting is one form carried through initial, intermediate and final phases |

## United States — federal

Federal clocks start at different moments — determination (SEC, banking), discovery (HIPAA, Safeguards), identification (FISMA, IRS) or reasonable belief (CIRCIA) — and most run in parallel with state law.

| Regime | Who must notify | Trigger + clock start | Deadline | Recipient | Content pointer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [SEC 8-K Item 1.05](../regulations/sec-cyber-disclosure.md) | US public companies (registrants) | Cybersecurity incident determined to be *material*; clock starts at materiality determination, which must be made without unreasonable delay after discovery | 4 business days from materiality determination | SEC (EDGAR 8-K filing — public) | Material aspects of nature, scope, and timing; material impact or reasonably likely material impact. National-security/public-safety delay only via written US Attorney General determination |
| [SEC Reg S-P](../regulations/us-sec-reg-sp-reg-sci.md) | Covered institutions: brokers and dealers, investment companies, SEC-registered advisers, registered transfer agents | Unauthorized access to or use of customer information; clock starts on becoming aware | Customer notice as soon as practicable, but not later than 30 days; service providers notify the covered institution no later than 72 hours | Affected individuals (service provider → covered institution upstream) | Incident-response programme requirements sit in the same rule |
| [SEC Reg SCI](../regulations/us-sec-reg-sp-reg-sci.md) | SCI entities | Reasonable basis to conclude an SCI event occurred | Notify the SEC immediately; written notice within 24 hours; updates until resolved; final report (or 30-day interim report) | SEC | Written notice and report content prescribed by the rule |
| [HIPAA Breach Notification Rule (individuals)](../regulations/hipaa.md) | Covered entity (business associate notifies CE, itself within 60 days of discovery) | Breach of unsecured PHI; clock starts on *discovery* (known, or would have been known with reasonable diligence — knowledge of workforce/agents is imputed) | Without unreasonable delay, no later than 60 calendar days from discovery | Affected individuals (written notice; substitute notice rules if contacts insufficient) | 45 CFR 164.404(c): what happened, PHI types, steps individuals should take, what entity is doing, contact info |
| [HIPAA (HHS + media)](../regulations/hipaa.md) | Covered entity | Same discovery clock | ≥500 individuals: HHS *and* — if more than 500 residents of one state/jurisdiction — prominent media, both within the same 60-day window. <500: HHS annual log within 60 days after calendar year end | HHS Office for Civil Rights (web portal); media outlet | Same core content as individual notice |
| [FTC Health Breach Notification Rule](../regulations/us-ftc-act-health-breach-rule.md) | Vendors of personal health records and related entities not covered by HIPAA; service providers notify the vendor | Breach of security of identifiable health information; clock starts on discovery | Without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery (individuals, media, service-provider-to-vendor notice); FTC at the same time where ≥500 individuals, otherwise an annual log within 60 days of year-end | Affected individuals; media; FTC | Rule-prescribed content; "breach" includes unauthorized disclosure, not only security failures |
| [FTC Safeguards Rule (GLBA non-bank)](../regulations/glba-ftc-safeguards.md) | Non-bank financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction | *Notification event*: acquisition of unencrypted customer information of ≥500 consumers; clock starts on discovery | 30 days from discovery | FTC (online form; disclosures are public) | Name/contact, description and date range of event, type of information, number of consumers, whether law enforcement requested delay |
| [US banking 36-hour rule (OCC/FRB/FDIC)](../regulations/us-banking-incident-notification-third-party.md) | Banking organizations | *Notification incident* — a computer-security incident that has materially disrupted or degraded (or is reasonably likely to) operations, business lines, or financial stability; clock starts at the determination, which may itself take a reasonable time | Regulator must *receive* notice as soon as possible and no later than 36 hours after the determination | Primary federal regulator (OCC, Federal Reserve, or FDIC) | No prescribed content or form; notice can be oral or written — the preamble treats same-day notice as the effective practice |
| [Bank service providers](../regulations/us-banking-incident-notification-third-party.md) | Bank service providers subject to the Bank Service Company Act | Material service disruption or degradation lasting four or more hours; clock starts at the provider's determination | As soon as possible after that determination — the agencies replaced "immediately" with "as soon as possible", so no fixed hour-count applies to the provider notice | Each affected banking organization customer | The bank's own 36-hour clock then runs from *its* determination |
| [NCUA 72-hour rule](../regulations/us-banking-incident-notification-third-party.md) | Federally insured credit unions | Reportable cyber incident; clock starts when the credit union *reasonably believes* it experienced one | NCUA must receive notice as soon as possible but no later than 72 hours — or within 72 hours of being notified by a third party, whichever is sooner | NCUA | Parallel to, not part of, the OCC/FRB/FDIC rule |
| [CIRCIA (duties not yet in force)](../regulations/us-circia.md) | "Covered entities" in the 16 critical-infrastructure sectors, as defined by the final rule | Covered cyber incident, on reasonable belief that one occurred; separately, making a ransom payment | Covered cyber incident: ≤72 hours. Ransom payment: ≤24 hours after payment, even if the attack is not a covered cyber incident. Supplemental reports "promptly" until the incident is concluded and fully mitigated | CISA (Sector Risk Management Agencies receive shared reports) | Reporting duties take effect on the dates prescribed in the final rule; until then CISA solicits voluntary reports. Data-preservation duty runs from the last required report |
| [FISMA / CISA federal incident reporting](../regulations/us-fisma-federal-cyber.md) | Federal executive-branch agencies (and contractors operating systems on their behalf) | Any incident, on identification by the agency's top-level CSIRT/SOC/IT function; separately, determination that an incident is *major* | Any incident: CISA within 1 hour of identification. Major incident: CISA and OMB OFCIO within 1 hour of the determination (M-25-04); Congress within 7 days of a reasonable basis to conclude it occurred | CISA; OMB; Congress | CISA's Federal Incident Notification Guidelines set the required attributes — see [../frameworks/nist-800-61-incident-handling.md](../frameworks/nist-800-61-incident-handling.md) |
| [FedRAMP incident reporting (CR26)](../frameworks/fedramp.md) | Cloud service providers with FedRAMP authorizations | Incident that affects, or is likely to affect, the confidentiality or integrity of federal customer data; severity set by the Potential Agency Impact (PAIN) rating | Class C: initial report 1 hour (PAIN-5/4/3), 24 hours (PAIN-2), 1 business day (PAIN-1); ongoing reports every 6/24 hours or every business day; final report 6 hours to 1 business day. Class B: initial 6 hours (PAIN-5/4/3) or 1 business day, final 3 business days | Agency customers via the authorization channel | For Class A, evaluating reportability and the final report are mandatory; initial and ongoing reports are recommended |
| [DFARS 252.204-7012 / CMMC](../frameworks/nist-800-171-cmmc.md) | Defense contractors and subcontractors handling covered defense information | Cyber incident affecting covered defense information or contract performance; clock starts on discovery | 72 hours from discovery | DoD via dibnet.dod.mil (a DoD-approved medium assurance certificate is needed *in advance*) | Preserve system images and packet capture for 90 days; submit isolated malicious software to DC3. A 2026 FAR proposal would add a 72-hour CUI incident report for civilian agencies |
| [IRS Publication 1075](../regulations/us-irs-pub-1075.md) | Agencies and contractors receiving federal tax information | Suspected or actual unauthorized disclosure or data incident involving FTI; clock starts on identification | Immediately, but no later than 24 hours after identification | Local TIGTA Field Division Special Agent-in-Charge, with a concurrent email to the IRS Office of Safeguards | Do not wait for an internal investigation to confirm FTI was involved |
| [CJIS Security Policy IR-6](../regulations/us-cjis-security-policy.md) | Agencies and contractors processing criminal justice information | Suspected incident; clock starts on discovery | Immediately, not to exceed 1 hour after discovery | The organization's incident-response capability, then the CSO / SIB Chief / IA Official and the FBI CJIS ISO | Supply-chain partners are informed under IR-6(3) |
| [NERC CIP-008-6 R4](../regulations/nerc-cip.md) | Registered Bulk Electric System entities | Reportable Cyber Security Incident, on determination; separately, an attempt to compromise | 1 hour from determination for a Reportable Cyber Security Incident; by the end of the next calendar day for an attempt to compromise; updates within 7 calendar days | E-ISAC and NCCIC (or successor) | Required attributes are set by the standard; an attempt-to-compromise report is not a breach report |
| [TSA and Coast Guard directives](../regulations/us-tsa-transportation-cyber.md) | TSA-designated pipeline, rail, aviation and transit owners/operators; regulated maritime facilities and vessels | Cybersecurity incident, on identification | TSA security directives: as soon as practicable but no later than 72 hours after identification (earlier directive versions required 24 hours, and the NPRM still proposes 24). Maritime: without delay to the NRC, or immediately under 33 CFR 6.16-1 | CISA; National Response Center; FBI and the Captain of the Port for maritime | Directive-specific reporting elements; the 24-hour proposal is live — check the current directive version |
| [FDA medical device postmarket reporting](../regulations/us-fda-medical-device-cybersecurity.md) | Device manufacturers | Uncontrolled cybersecurity risk, correction or removal, or a reportable malfunction/serious injury; clock starts on initiation of the action or on becoming aware | 21 CFR 806.10 corrections and removals: 10 working days from initiation. MDR: 30 calendar days from becoming aware, or 5 work days where FDA requested it or remedial action is needed. Enforcement discretion for uncontrolled risk requires customer notification within 30 days of learning of the vulnerability and a validated fix distributed within 60 days | FDA | The 30/60-day route also requires no known serious adverse events or deaths and active ISAO participation |
| [DOJ bulk sensitive data rule](../regulations/us-doj-bulk-data-rule.md) | US persons engaging in covered data transactions | Known or suspected breach of the contractual onward-transfer bar; separately, rejecting a prohibited data-brokerage offer | Within 14 days of learning of the breach of the clause (§ 202.302(b)); within 14 days of affirmatively rejecting a prohibited transaction (§ 202.1104) | DOJ National Security Division | These are filings about contract and transaction events, not security-incident reports — but they land on the same team |

## United States — state

Every state has a breach statute; the variables are the definition of personal information, the harm threshold, the outer deadline and the regulator-notice trigger. The roll-up row is the starting point; the illustrative rows show the spread. Build the per-incident grid from [../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md), not from memory.

| Regime | Who must notify | Trigger + clock start | Deadline | Recipient | Content pointer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [State breach statutes (all 50 + DC and territories)](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Data owner/licensee (service providers and maintainers notify the owner) | Breach of state-defined personal information; clock start varies — discovery, determination, or the conclusion that misuse is likely | "Most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay" everywhere; roughly half the states add an outer limit of 30, 45 or 60 days; Puerto Rico gives 10 days to the regulator | Affected residents; AG or state regulator above thresholds; consumer reporting agencies above thresholds | State-specific content rules; several states prescribe the notice format and prohibit certain delay |
| [California §1798.82 / CCPA](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Businesses holding California residents' data | Breach of unencrypted personal information; discovery-based | 30 calendar days from discovery or notification (SB 446, effective 1 Jan 2026); sample notice to the AG within 15 calendar days of notifying individuals where >500 residents | Affected residents; California Attorney General | Prescribed "Notice of Data Breach" template (What Happened? / What Information Was Involved? …). Note CCPA §1798.150 gives a *private right of action* with statutory damages $100–$750 per consumer per incident where the breach follows a failure to maintain reasonable security — see [../regulations/us-state-privacy.md](../regulations/us-state-privacy.md) |
| [Florida §501.171](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Covered entities holding Florida residents' data | Unauthorized *access* to personal information; clock starts on determination | 30 days from determination; Department of Legal Affairs within 30 days where ≥500 residents (15-day extension for good cause in writing) | Affected residents; Department of Legal Affairs; CRAs where >1,000 | Penalties accrue per day; a harm waiver requires a documented determination |
| [Texas §521.053](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Persons conducting business in Texas | Breach of sensitive personal information; clock starts on determination | 60th day after determination; AG within 30 days of determination where ≥250 Texas residents | Affected individuals; Texas AG (electronic form; the AG publishes a public list); CRAs where >10,000 | Encrypted data is in scope if the intruder holds the key; no harm threshold |
| [Maryland Com. Law §14-3504](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Business owning or licensing the data (maintainer notifies the owner within 10 days) | Breach of personal information; discovery or notification | 45 days from discovery/notification; AG notice comes **before** individual notice, at any size | Maryland AG first, then affected individuals | No-notice determinations must be retained for 3 years |
| [Vermont 9 V.S.A. §2435](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Data collectors | Breach of personally identifiable information; discovery-based | 45 days from discovery to consumers; preliminary description to the AG (DFR for licensees) within 14 business days of discovery or of consumer notice, whichever is sooner | Vermont AG or DFR; affected consumers | A pre-sworn security-policy filing shortens the AG step to "before consumer notice"; no-harm determinations must be filed with an explanation |
| [Pennsylvania BPINA](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) | Entities maintaining Pennsylvania residents' data; public-sector bodies on a shorter clock | Breach; clock starts on determination | Without unreasonable delay after determination; **7 business days** for state agencies, counties, public schools and municipalities | Affected residents; AG concurrently where >500 residents | State-agency contractors notify the agency's CISO on the contract's timetable |
| [NYDFS Part 500 §500.17](../regulations/us-nydfs-part-500.md) | DFS-licensed financial services companies | Cybersecurity incident meeting the §500.17(a) criteria; separately, making an extortion payment | 72 hours to notify DFS of a cybersecurity incident; 24 hours to notify an extortion payment, plus a written explanation within 30 days | New York DFS | The 15 April annual certification or acknowledgment of non-compliance is a separate filing clock |
| [NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law](../regulations/us-naic-insurance-data-security.md) | Insurance licensees in adopting states | Determination that a Cybersecurity Event occurred | As promptly as possible and no later than 72 hours after the determination (§6A); state variants differ — Virginia uses 3 business days | State insurance Commissioner; consumer notice runs under the state's general breach statute (§6C) | Licensees must also investigate and retain records of the event |
| [California SB 53 (frontier AI)](../regulations/us-state-ai-laws.md) | Frontier AI developers | *Critical safety incident*; clock starts on discovery | 15 days to OES from discovery; 24 hours to an appropriate authority where there is imminent risk of death or serious injury | California Office of Emergency Services | Large frontier developers also transmit catastrophic-risk assessment summaries to OES every 3 months |
| [Student-data laws (IL SOPPA, NY Ed Law §2-d)](../regulations/us-ferpa-education-privacy.md) | Ed-tech operators, contractors and the schools/agencies they serve | Breach of covered student information; determination or discovery | Illinois: operator → school ≤30 calendar days after determining a breach; school → parents ≤30 calendar days. ## Asia-Pacific

The region's clocks are the tightest in the matrix — several run in hours, and financial-sector rules almost always beat the privacy clock for the same event.

| Regime | Who must notify | Trigger + clock start | Deadline | Recipient | Content pointer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Australia Privacy Act NDB scheme](../regulations/australia-privacy-act.md) | APP entities | Suspected eligible data breach triggers a reasonable and expeditious assessment (all reasonable steps to complete within 30 days, s 26WH); the notification clock runs from reasonable grounds to believe | Statement to the Commissioner and notification of individuals as soon as practicable (ss 26WK–26WL) | OAIC; affected individuals | The 30-day assessment window is a ceiling on investigating, not a notification deadline |
| [APRA CPS 234 / CPS 230](../regulations/australia-apra-cps-234-230.md) | APRA-regulated entities (banks, insurers, superannuation funds) | CPS 234: material information security incident, on awareness. CPS 230: material operational risk incident; separately, a disruption to a critical operation outside tolerance | 72 hours (material information security incident, and material operational risk incident); 24 hours (disruption outside tolerance); 10 business days (material control weakness that cannot be remediated in a timely manner); 20 business days for the CPS 230 notification set | APRA | CPS 234 notifications need not be repeated under CPS 230 for the same event |
| [SOCI Act and Cyber Security Act](../regulations/australia-soci-cyber-security-act.md) | Responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets; reporting business entities for ransomware payments | Cyber security incident with a *significant impact on availability* or a *relevant impact*; clock starts on becoming aware. Ransomware: on making or becoming aware of a payment | 12 hours (significant impact on availability — oral report allowed, written record in the approved form within 84 hours); 72 hours (other relevant impact — written record within 48 hours if given orally); ransomware payment report within 72 hours of the payment | ASD; designated Commonwealth body for ransomware payment reports | The CIRMP annual report (within 90 days of financial-year end, board-approved) and 30-day register updates are separate clocks |
| [New Zealand Privacy Act](../regulations/new-zealand-privacy-act.md) | Agencies subject to the Act | Notifiable privacy breach; clock starts on becoming aware | As soon as practicable (ss 114–115); the OPC's stated expectation is within 72 hours | Privacy Commissioner; affected individuals | The Commissioner's online notification tool doubles as the risk-assessment aid |
| [Japan APPI](../regulations/japan-appi-cyber.md) | Personal information handling business operators | Reportable leak categories; the clock starts when the operator "knew" — for a corporation, when *any department* became aware | Preliminary report to the PPC promptly (guideline yardstick: roughly 3–5 days from awareness); final report within 30 days, or 60 days where a malicious act is suspected (Enforcement Rules Art. 8); individuals notified promptly (Rules Art. 10) | Personal Information Protection Commission; affected individuals | Reportable classes include sensitive data, risk of financial harm, unlawful intent, and incidents affecting more than 1,000 individuals |
| [China PIPL / DSL / CSL](../regulations/china-pipl-dsl-csl.md) | CII operators; central and state organs; all other network operators | Cybersecurity incident, per the national incident-reporting measures | CII: at once, no later than 1 hour. Central and state organs: 2 hours to their department's cyber office. All others: 4 hours to the provincial CAC. Major/particularly major incidents escalate to the national CAC within 30 minutes (CII chain) or 1 hour | Protection work department and public security (CII); provincial or national CAC | Sector rules may add reports; suspected crimes go to public security. ## Clock-start nuances — the traps

**GDPR "awareness."** The 72 hours run from when the controller has a reasonable degree of certainty that a breach of personal data has occurred — not from full forensic confirmation, and not from first alert of a mere anomaly. A short investigation to establish whether a breach occurred is acceptable; the investigation period itself is scrutinized. The clock is calendar time: weekends and holidays count. If a processor detects the breach, the controller is generally treated as aware once the processor informs it — contract for tight processor-to-controller notification (hours, not days).

**NIS2 staged reporting.** The 24-hour early warning is intentionally thin (suspected malicious cause, possible cross-border impact) — do not hold it back to polish details. The 72-hour notification updates it with an initial assessment; the final report is due within one month of the 72-hour notification. Member-state transpositions may add national specifics (portals, thresholds, registration clocks); check the implementing law of each state where you are regulated — [../regulations/eu-nis2-implementing-and-transposition.md](../regulations/eu-nis2-implementing-and-transposition.md).

**DORA layering.** DORA's clocks run from *classification* of the incident as major (initial report), so your classification process is effectively part of the deadline. DORA reporting is in addition to, not instead of, GDPR notification when personal data is involved. The 4h/24h/72h/1-month figures come from Regulation 2025/301, not DORA's own articles — see [../regulations/eu-dora-technical-standards.md](../regulations/eu-dora-technical-standards.md).

**HIPAA "discovery" is imputed.** Discovery occurs when any workforce member or agent (other than the person who committed the breach) knows or should have known. The 60 days are an outer limit — "without unreasonable delay" is the operative standard, and OCR has penalized entities that treated 60 days as an entitlement. Presumption: an impermissible use/disclosure of unsecured PHI *is* a breach unless a documented risk assessment demonstrates low probability of compromise.

**SEC: two clocks, not one.** The 4-business-day clock starts at the materiality *determination*, but registrants may not unreasonably delay making that determination. Document the determination process and date. Materiality follows securities-law standards (quantitative and qualitative; consider aggregation of related incidents). Item 1.05 filings are public — coordinate with counsel on content that could aid attackers.

**US state laws: 50+ different definitions.** "Personal information" scope, encryption safe harbors, risk-of-harm thresholds, AG-notice triggers, and outer deadlines all vary. A multi-state breach requires a state-by-state grid — build it per incident from current statutes, not from memory. See [../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md](../regulations/us-state-breach-notification-laws.md) and [../regulations/us-state-privacy.md](../regulations/us-state-privacy.md).

**Banking 36-hour rule.** The clock starts at the *determination* that a notification incident occurred, and the bar is operational disruption — no personal data need be involved. The rule expects notification well before facts are complete; a phone call to your examiner satisfies it.

**PCI is contractual.** No statute sets the deadline — your merchant agreement and the card brands' rules do. Missing it risks fines passed through by the acquirer, mandatory PFI forensic investigation, and potential loss of processing ability. Treat brand notification as a same-day action item.

**Determination vs discovery vs awareness vs reasonable belief.** Four different starting guns, often for the same event. *Discovery* (HIPAA, FTC Safeguards, most state statutes, South Africa) starts earliest and is the least forgiving — imputed knowledge counts. *Awareness* (GDPR, NIS2, CER, SOCI, Saudi PDPL, Korea) allows a short verification step but not a full forensic investigation. *Determination* (SEC, the banking 36-hour rule, Florida, Texas, Colorado, NAIC, the UK's 2027 regime) gives you an assessment period but makes the assessment itself auditable — an undocumented or slow determination is the finding. *Reasonable belief* (CIRCIA, NCUA) sits between them. Record which standard each applicable regime uses before the incident, because you cannot reconstruct it credibly afterwards.

**Business days, working days and calendar days are not interchangeable.** Brazil's 3 days and Virginia's NAIC variant are *business* days; Colombia's 15 days to the RNBD and APRA's 10- and 20-day notifications are business days; the UK's 2027 final report runs in *working* days (30, with a 60-working-day absolute limit); FDA Part 806 runs in *working* days and MDR in *calendar* days; most FedRAMP clocks are hours or business days depending on PAIN rating. GDPR, NIS2, HIPAA and the state statutes that set outer limits run in calendar days — a Friday-evening discovery burns the weekend. DORA has an explicit rule for the edge case: a deadline falling on a weekend or bank holiday moves to noon of the next working day, except for initial and intermediate reports from credit institutions, CCPs, trading-venue operators and NIS2 essential/important entities.

**Regulator before individuals — and sometimes the reverse.** Maryland requires AG notice *before* individual notice at any size. Vermont requires a preliminary AG description within 14 business days, ahead of consumer notice. South Korea's decree notifies data subjects within 72 hours while the PIPC report is threshold-based. Brazil runs both on the same 3 business days. Israel's authority may order individual notification only after consulting the national cyber directorate. Sequence the notifications deliberately: sending individual notices first can breach a statute, and sending them late after a regulator filing invites a second finding.

**Parallel clocks for one incident.** A single event routinely runs several clocks from different moments: a Singapore bank owes MAS at 1 hour and the PDPC at 3 days from assessment; an Indian fiduciary owes CERT-In at 6 hours and the Data Protection Board at 72; a UAE bank owes CBUAE at 4 hours and the ADGM or DIFC regulator on a separate standard; a US defense contractor may owe DoD at 72 hours, CISA under CIRCIA once that rule is in force, and its state AGs on top. Maintain one incident record with a per-regulator clock table rather than a single "notification deadline" field — [../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md](../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md).

**Assessment windows that precede the notification clock.** Several regimes give you a bounded investigation before any deadline starts, and the window is a ceiling, not an allowance: Australia's NDB scheme expects all reasonable steps to complete the assessment within 30 days, then notification as soon as practicable; Singapore requires a "reasonable and expeditious" assessment before the 3-calendar-day clock starts; HIPAA's risk assessment rebuts the breach presumption but does not pause discovery; Colorado and Florida hinge on "determination" after sufficient evidence. Diarise the assessment window itself, document when it opened and closed, and treat an assessment that runs to its outer edge as a decision you must justify.

**Regimes that are not yet in force.** CIRCIA's reporting duties begin on the final rule's effective date; the UK's Cyber Security and Resilience Bill and the 18 March 2027 FCA/PRA regime are pending; the EHDS applies generally from 26 March 2027; the UAE PDPL's breach period awaits the Executive Regulations; Korea's possible-leak duty applies from 11 September 2026. Build the playbook now, but do not report to a regime that cannot yet receive the report — and re-check status through [../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md).

## Common errors

1. **Starting the SEC clock at detection** instead of the materiality determination — or, conversely, slow-walking the materiality determination without documented justification.
2. **Treating GDPR's 72 hours as business hours** or waiting for complete forensics. File a phased initial notification; supplement later.
3. **Sending a full report as the NIS2 24-hour early warning** (late because you over-scoped it) or treating the early warning as discharging the 72-hour obligation.
4. **Ignoring upstream contractual clocks**: processor-to-controller (GDPR), business associate-to-covered entity (HIPAA), service provider-to-customer (Reg S-P, state laws, banking rule), intermediary-to-controlling-organisation (Singapore) obligations are often shorter than the regulatory deadline and are frequently breached first.
5. **Assuming an encryption safe harbor applies everywhere.** Safe harbors differ by regime (HIPAA "unsecured PHI" guidance, state statutes, GDPR Art. 34(3)(a), Singapore's pre-existing technological measures) and usually fail if the key was also compromised — Texas expressly keeps encrypted data in scope when the intruder holds the key.
6. **Handling only one regime.** A ransomware event at a US-listed EU bank can simultaneously trigger GDPR, NIS2 or DORA, SEC Item 1.05, state breach laws, and card-brand rules — with clocks starting at four different moments. Run the applicability check first: [../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md).
7. **Forgetting the payment-report clocks.** Ransom and extortion payments carry their own deadlines independent of the incident report — NYDFS at 24 hours, CIRCIA at 24 hours once in force, Australia's Cyber Security Act at 72 hours — and Swiss guidance expects FDPIC notification *before* a payment decision is taken.
8. **Forgetting non-regulatory notifications**: cyber insurers (policy conditions often require prompt notice before incurring response costs), certification bodies (HITRUST's 60-day security-event clock), customers under contract SLAs, and law-enforcement coordination (which may permit — but rarely requires — delay of individual notice).
9. **No evidence of the timeline itself.** Regulators ask when you became aware and how you determined it. Timestamp awareness, classification, assessment and determination decisions in the incident record as they happen.
