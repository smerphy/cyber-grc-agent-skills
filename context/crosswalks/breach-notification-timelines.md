# Breach & Incident Notification Timelines

Deadline matrix across major regimes. Use this to build notification decision trees and incident-response playbooks; then verify each applicable regime against official text and current guidance before filing anything. Clock-start definitions differ materially between regimes — see the nuance section, it is where most real-world failures happen.

Scope note: "breach" below covers both personal-data breaches (GDPR, HIPAA, state laws) and operational/security incidents (NIS2, DORA, SEC, banking rule). A single event frequently triggers several regimes at once with different clocks.

## The matrix

| CIRCIA (US critical infrastructure) | Covered entities in the 16 critical-infrastructure sectors (per final rule) | *Covered cyber incident*; ransom payment separately | 72 hours for covered incidents; 24 hours for ransom payments; supplemental reports on new info — **rule finalization/effective dates: verify current status** | CISA (web portal) | Per rule: incident description, vulnerabilities, TTPs, impact, ransom details; substantially-similar reporting to another federal regime may satisfy — verify; see [../regulations/circia.md](../regulations/circia.md) |

| Singapore MAS notices | MAS-regulated financial institutions | *Relevant* IT security incident or major system malfunction | **1 hour** from discovery; root-cause and impact report within 14 days | MAS | Sectoral overlay on top of PDPA; see [../regulations/singapore.md](../regulations/singapore.md) |
| Japan APPI | Business operators handling personal data | Four reportable classes (special care-required data, financial-harm risk, wrongful-intent incidents, >1,000 individuals); awareness-based | Prompt initial report (guidance: ~3–5 days) → final report within 30 days (60 for wrongful-intent cases) | PPC + affected individuals | Two-stage content; see [../regulations/japan.md](../regulations/japan.md) |
| South Korea PIPA | Personal information controllers | Breach meeting thresholds (scale/sensitive data/unauthorized access — verify decree) | 72 hours to affected individuals and PIPC | PIPC + affected data subjects | Prescribed elements; see [../regulations/south-korea.md](../regulations/south-korea.md) |
| India DPDP Act | Data fiduciaries | *Any* personal data breach — no statutory harm threshold | Per DPDP Rules: individuals without delay; detailed report to the Board within 72 hours (extendable) — verify final Rules | Data Protection Board of India + each affected data principal | See [../regulations/india-dpdp.md](../regulations/india-dpdp.md) |
| India CERT-In directions | Service providers, intermediaries, companies operating in India | Specified incident types (20-item list incl. data breaches, ransomware) | **6 hours** from noticing | CERT-In | Separate from and additional to DPDP duties; see [../regulations/india-dpdp.md](../regulations/india-dpdp.md) |
| China PIPL | Personal information processors | Leakage, tampering, or loss of personal information | Remedial measures + **prompt** notification (timelines tightened by incident-reporting measures — verify; severe incidents expected within hours) | Regulator (CAC/sectoral) + affected individuals (waivable if harm effectively avoided, unless ordered) | See [../regulations/china.md](../regulations/china.md) |
| Brazil LGPD | Controllers | Security incident that may cause *relevant risk or damage* to data subjects | 3 business days from knowledge (ANPD 2024 regulation) | ANPD + affected data subjects | Prescribed content, incident register; see [../regulations/brazil-lgpd.md](../regulations/brazil-lgpd.md) |

## Clock-start nuances — the traps

**GDPR "awareness."** The 72 hours run from when the controller has a reasonable degree of certainty that a breach of personal data has occurred — not from full forensic confirmation, and not from first alert of a mere anomaly. A short investigation to establish whether a breach occurred is acceptable; the investigation period itself is scrutinized. The clock is calendar time: weekends and holidays count. If a processor detects the breach, the controller is generally treated as aware once the processor informs it — contract for tight processor-to-controller notification (hours, not days).

**NIS2 staged reporting.** The 24-hour early warning is intentionally thin (suspected malicious cause, possible cross-border impact) — do not hold it back to polish details. The 72-hour notification updates it with an initial assessment; the final report is due within one month of the 72-hour notification. Member-state transpositions may add national specifics (portals, thresholds); check the implementing law of each state where you are regulated.

**DORA layering.** DORA's clocks run from *classification* of the incident as major (initial report) — classification itself is governed by criteria in an RTS, so your classification process is effectively part of the deadline. DORA reporting is in addition to, not instead of, GDPR notification when personal data is involved. The 4h/24h/72h/1-month figures come from delegated regulation, not DORA's own articles — verify current text.

**HIPAA "discovery" is imputed.** Discovery occurs when any workforce member or agent (other than the person who committed the breach) knows or should have known. The 60 days are an outer limit — "without unreasonable delay" is the operative standard, and OCR has penalized entities that treated 60 days as an entitlement. Presumption: an impermissible use/disclosure of unsecured PHI *is* a breach unless a documented risk assessment demonstrates low probability of compromise.

**SEC: two clocks, not one.** The 4-business-day clock starts at the materiality *determination*, but registrants may not unreasonably delay making that determination. Document the determination process and date. Materiality follows securities-law standards (quantitative and qualitative; consider aggregation of related incidents). Item 1.05 filings are public — coordinate with counsel on content that could aid attackers.

**US state laws: 50+ different definitions.** "Personal information" scope, encryption safe harbors, risk-of-harm thresholds, AG-notice triggers, and outer deadlines all vary. A multi-state breach requires a state-by-state grid — build it per incident from current statutes, not from memory. See [../regulations/us-state-privacy.md](../regulations/us-state-privacy.md).

**Banking 36-hour rule.** The clock starts at the *determination* that a notification incident occurred, and the bar is operational disruption — no personal data need be involved. The rule expects notification well before facts are complete; a phone call to your examiner satisfies it.

**PCI is contractual.** No statute sets the deadline — your merchant agreement and the card brands' rules do. Missing it risks fines passed through by the acquirer, mandatory PFI forensic investigation, and potential loss of processing ability. Treat brand notification as a same-day action item.

**NYDFS runs on determination, like the SEC.** The 72 hours start when the entity *determines* a reportable cybersecurity event occurred (e.g., material-harm likelihood or a notice obligation to any other body) — determination discipline and its documentation are part of compliance. The extortion-payment clocks (24h notice, 30-day explanation of why payment was necessary and alternatives considered) run separately from the event notice.

**CIRCIA is a moving target.** The 72/24-hour duties bind only once the final rule applies to you as a covered entity; but the substantially-similar-reporting mechanics mean your SEC, banking, or sector filings interact with it. Confirm the rule's current status and covered-entity criteria before wiring clocks into the playbook.

**Singapore is two-step by design.** The 3-day clock starts at the *determination* that a breach is notifiable, and the preceding assessment must itself be expeditious (guidance: ~30 days). Regulators examine both durations — a fast filing after a leisurely assessment still fails.

**Japan is two-stage like NIS2.** File the thin prompt report within days without waiting for forensics; the 30/60-day final report carries the detail. Missing the initial report because the investigation is "not done" is the standard failure.

## Common errors

1. **Starting the SEC clock at detection** instead of the materiality determination — or, conversely, slow-walking the materiality determination without documented justification.
2. **Treating GDPR's 72 hours as business hours** or waiting for complete forensics. File a phased initial notification; supplement later.
3. **Sending a full report as the NIS2 24-hour early warning** (late because you over-scoped it) or treating the early warning as discharging the 72-hour obligation.
4. **Ignoring upstream contractual clocks**: processor-to-controller (GDPR), business associate-to-covered entity (HIPAA), service provider-to-customer (state laws, banking rule) obligations are often shorter than the regulatory deadline and are frequently breached first.
5. **Assuming an encryption safe harbor applies everywhere.** Safe harbors differ by regime (HIPAA "unsecured PHI" guidance, state statutes, GDPR Art. 34(3)(a)) and usually fail if the key was also compromised.
6. **Handling only one regime.** A ransomware event at a US-listed EU bank can simultaneously trigger GDPR, NIS2 or DORA, SEC Item 1.05, state breach laws, and card-brand rules — with clocks starting at four different moments. Run the applicability check first: [../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md).
7. **Forgetting non-regulatory notifications**: cyber insurers (policy conditions often require prompt notice before incurring response costs), customers under contract SLAs, and law-enforcement coordination (which may permit — but rarely requires — delay of individual notice).
8. **No evidence of the timeline itself.** Regulators ask when you became aware and how you determined it. Timestamp awareness, classification, and determination decisions in the incident record as they happen.
