# UK Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018 and the Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill (UK NIS / CS&R Bill)

## At a glance

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Instrument | The Network and Information Systems Regulations 2018, SI 2018/506 (in force 10 May 2018) — the UK's retained implementation of EU Directive 2016/1148 (NIS1), amended by SI 2018/629 and, post-Brexit, by SI 2019/653, SI 2019/1444, SI 2020/1245, SI 2021/1461, the Health and Care Act 2022, SI 2023/424 and SI 2024/706; reform Bill: Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill, introduced 12 November 2025 |
| Status (Sept 2026) | NIS Regulations 2018 in force and unchanged in substance; the Bill is **not yet an Act** — it passed the Commons (report stage and third reading 16 June 2026), had Lords second reading 14 July 2026, completed Lords Grand Committee on 7 September 2026 (reprinted as HL Bill 49) and awaits Lords report stage, listed for 26 October 2026; most new duties then depend on secondary legislation and a business adjustment period |
| Lead department / regulators | DSIT owns the policy; Schedule 1 designates sector competent authorities for operators of essential services (OES) — Secretaries of State, Ofgem, the CAA, Ofcom, the Drinking Water Quality Regulator for Scotland and the devolved administrations; DSIT counts 12 NIS regulators in all, the ICO included; the ICO (Information Commission from 30 September 2026) regulates relevant digital service providers (RDSP) and, under the Bill, relevant managed service providers (RMSP); GCHQ is designated single point of contact and CSIRT (regs 4–5), delivered through the NCSC |
| Who is covered today | OES in energy, transport, health, drinking water and digital infrastructure meeting Schedule 2 thresholds; RDSPs (online marketplaces, online search engines, cloud computing services) that are not micro/small enterprises |
| Who the Bill adds | Data centres (≥1 MW rated IT load; enterprise data centres ≥10 MW), medium and large managed service providers, large load controllers (≥300 MW), and regulator-designated critical suppliers |
| Core duties | Appropriate and proportionate technical and organisational security measures (regs 10, 12); incident notification to the regulator within 72 hours of awareness (regs 11, 12); registration/notification and UK representative duties (regs 8, 8A, 14, 14A) |
| Bill incident clock | Initial notification within 24 hours of awareness, fuller report within 72 hours, NCSC informed at the same time; customer notification duties for data centres, RDSPs and RMSPs |
| Penalties | Today: three bands capped at £1m / £8.5m / £17m (reg 18(6)). Bill: two bands — up to £17m or 4% of worldwide turnover (higher band) and £10m or 2% (standard band), whichever is higher |

| Relationship to neighbours | UK counterpart of EU NIS2 (the April 2025 policy statement commits to aligning "where appropriate"); sits alongside UK GDPR breach duties, the Telecommunications (Security) Act 2021 (telecoms providers are carved out of NIS) and FSMA 2023 critical third parties for finance |

## What it is

The NIS Regulations 2018 transposed the EU NIS Directive into UK law and are the UK's only cross-sector cyber security legislation. They place two duties on in-scope organisations — take appropriate and proportionate measures to manage risks to the security of the network and information systems their service relies on, and notify incidents with a significant (OES) or substantial (RDSP) impact — and give sector regulators information, inspection, enforcement-notice and penalty powers. After Brexit the Regulations were retained and amended (notably SI 2020/1245 from 31 December 2020, which rewrote the penalty and enforcement machinery in regs 18–19B and added the OES nominated-person duty in reg 8A, and SI 2019/653 and SI 2019/1444 from 20 January 2021, which stripped out EU cooperation mechanics and inserted the UK-representative duty for non-UK digital service providers in reg 14A). Two statutory post-implementation reviews (2020 and 2022) found the regime raised standards but was implemented inconsistently across sectors and needed updating for new threats and technologies.

The Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Bill is the government's response. It does not replace the 2018 Regulations; it amends them, adds Secretary of State functions — a statement of strategic priorities, regulation-making powers and a code of practice — and creates a separate national security directions regime (in the HL Bill 49 print of 7 September 2026 these are Parts 2, 3 and 4 of five Parts, 61 clauses and two Schedules; clause 61 would cite the Act as the Cyber Security and Resilience (Network and Information Systems) Act 2026). The government frames it around three pillars: expanded scope, more effective regulators (24-hour incident reporting, cost recovery, information sharing, simplified penalties, strategic priorities), and enabling resilience (delegated powers to update the regime and powers of direction). Data infrastructure was designated critical national infrastructure on 12 September 2024 and the Bill formalises its regulation.

## Who it covers / Scope

**Operators of essential services (Part 3 of the Regulations).** A person is *deemed designated* as an OES if it provides a Schedule 2 essential service that relies on network and information systems and meets the threshold for that service (reg 8(1)); it must notify the competent authority in writing within three months of falling within scope (reg 8(2), (11)). Competent authorities may also designate below-threshold providers where an incident is likely to have significant disruptive effects, weighing users, sector dependency, impact, market share, geography, substitutability and national security (reg 8(3)–(4)), and must keep and review their OES list at least biennially (reg 8(8)–(10)). Integrated care boards are deemed OES in England (reg 8(2A)). Telecoms providers subject to sections 105A–105C of the Communications Act 2003 are excluded (reg 8(1A)). Illustrative Schedule 2 thresholds:

| Subsector | Threshold (selected) |
|---|---|
| Electricity | Supply to >250,000 final customers, or supply plus generation ≥2 GW; transmission/distribution with potential to disrupt >250,000 customers; interconnectors ≥1 GW |
| Gas | Supply to >250,000 final customers (GB); transmission/distribution with potential to disrupt >250,000 customers; storage/LNG/interconnector capacity >20 million m³/day |
| Oil | Upstream pipelines with throughput >3,000,000 tonnes of oil equivalent per year (plus refining, storage and processing thresholds) |
| Air transport | Aerodromes with >10 million annual terminal passengers; air traffic services at such airports; carriers with >30% of passengers at such an airport and >10 million passengers UK-wide |
| Water transport | Ports/harbour authorities/port facilities/vessel traffic services at ports with >10 million passengers or defined UK shares of ro-ro, lo-lo, liquid bulk or biomass freight; shipping companies with >5 million tonnes of annual UK freight and >30% of freight at such a port, or >30% of passengers at a >10 million-passenger port |
| Rail | Any operator of a mainline railway asset in GB (excluding heritage, private freight-only and light-rail networks) and any railway undertaking in NI; high-speed rail operators; metros, trams and light rail with >50 million annual passenger journeys; Channel Tunnel train operators and the Channel Fixed Link infrastructure manager |
| Health | NHS trusts and foundation trusts (England), local health boards/NHS trusts (Wales), health boards and the Common Services Agency (Scotland), HSC trusts (NI) |
| Drinking water | Supply of potable water to ≥200,000 people |
| Digital infrastructure | TLD registries serving ≥14 billion UK queries in any 168 hours; DNS resolvers serving ≥500,000 UK IP addresses; authoritative DNS hosting ≥100,000 UK-registered domains; IXPs with ≥30% UK market share — irrespective of place of establishment |

**Relevant digital service providers (Part 4).** An RDSP provides an online marketplace, online search engine or cloud computing service in the UK, has its head office in the UK or a nominated UK representative, and is not a micro or small enterprise under Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC (reg 1(3)(e)) — ICO guidance: fewer than 50 staff and turnover and/or balance sheet below €10 million exempts, assessed at group level. Non-UK providers offering digital services in the UK must nominate a UK representative within three months (reg 14A). OES with head offices outside the UK must nominate a UK person to act for them (reg 8A; automatic for energy and digital infrastructure, on notice for other sectors).

**Bill additions (all commenced by secondary legislation after Royal Assent).**

| New category | Test | Regulator |
|---|---|---|
| Data centres | Data infrastructure becomes a NIS sector and data centre services an essential service; in scope at rated IT load ≥1 MW, or ≥10 MW for enterprise data centres run solely for the owner's own IT; applies to Crown facilities, excluding intelligence-agency and SECRET/TOP SECRET workloads; notify within 3 months of designation | Ofcom |
| Relevant managed service providers | Provide, under contract, ongoing management of a customer's IT systems (support, maintenance, monitoring, administration) by connecting to or accessing the customer's network and information systems, on-premises or remotely, whether or not established in the UK; not small/micro; public-authority-overseen non-commercial entities exempt; excludes data centre services and public electronic communications networks/services; OT-only providers, installers, consultancies and software-as-product vendors are out; register within 3 months of commencement | ICO / Information Commission |
| Large load controllers | Potential electrical control of ≥300 MW across relevant energy smart appliances; narrowed in Lords Grand Committee to controllers that also carry on activities for system-balancing purposes; an intermediary authorised to adjust or process load-control signals is itself treated as a load controller; notify within 3 months of falling within scope | DESNZ and Ofgem jointly |
| Designated critical suppliers | Regulator may designate a supplier of goods or services to an OES/RDSP/RMSP it regulates where the supply relies on network and information systems and an incident could disrupt the regulated service with significant impact on the economy or society, after considering substitutability and other regulatory cover, consulting the supplier and coordinating with other regulators; small and micro digital/managed service providers can be designated; appeal to the First-tier Tribunal | Regulator of the customer it supplies |

The Bill also gives the Secretary of State an affirmative-procedure power to add services or activities that are or have become essential to the economy or society, subject to consultation ("futureproofing", 11 delegated powers).

## Core obligations

| Duty | NIS Regulations 2018 (current law) | Cyber Security and Resilience Bill (as proposed; detail in secondary legislation) |
|---|---|---|
| Security measures — OES | Appropriate and proportionate technical and organisational measures to manage risks to the security of systems the essential service relies on; measures to prevent and minimise incident impact with a view to continuity; state-of-the-art level appropriate to risk; regard to competent authority guidance (reg 10) | Same core duties extended to data centres and large load controllers; supply-chain duties to be clarified in regulations; a statutory code of practice; power to introduce security and resilience requirements for all regulated entities |
| Security measures — RDSP | Identify and take appropriate and proportionate measures for the systems relied on to provide the service in the UK, taking into account the five elements of Article 2 of EU Regulation 2018/151 (systems and facilities security, incident handling, business continuity, monitoring/auditing/testing, international standards) (reg 12(1)–(2)) | Duty clarified to cover third-party systems the service relies on and availability, authenticity, integrity and confidentiality (not only continuity); the Regulation 2018/151 reference is removed when replacement requirements are made; cloud computing definition expanded (broad remote access, on-demand, self-service) |
| Incident notification — OES | Notify the competent authority in writing of any incident with a significant impact on continuity, judged on users affected, duration and geography; content: operator name and services, time, duration, nature and impact, cross-border impact, other helpful information; without undue delay and no later than 72 hours after awareness; competent authority shares with the CSIRT (reg 11) | Two-stage reporting: initial notification within 24 hours of awareness (entity name, service, brief details), fuller report within 72 hours (time, whether ongoing, nature, whether caused by an incident at another regulated entity, actual/likely impact); NCSC sighted at the same time; reportable events widen to incidents that adversely affect the operation or security of relied-on systems with significant actual or likely UK impact — capturing ransomware, pre-positioning and near-miss compromises before service disruption; data centres report significant impacts on system security, service continuity or the UK generally |
| Incident notification — RDSP | Notify the ICO of incidents with a substantial impact, no later than 72 hours after first awareness, where the RDSP has the information to assess impact; parameters from Article 3 of Regulation 2018/151 plus ICO thresholds: >750,000 user-hours unavailable; loss of integrity, authenticity or confidentiality affecting >15,000 UK users; risk to public safety, security or life; material damage to one UK user >£850,000 (reg 12(3)–(7); ICO Guide to NIS) | Same 24h/72h structure; significance factors add impact on users' network and information systems and on the economy or society; after the full report, RDSPs, RMSPs and data centres must identify and notify customers likely to have been adversely affected, with details and reasons |
| Dependency reporting | An OES relying on an RDSP must notify its competent authority without undue delay of significant continuity impacts caused by an incident at the RDSP (reg 12(9)) | Full report must state whether the incident was caused by an incident affecting another regulated entity |
| Registration / representatives | RDSPs register name, head-office or representative address and contact details with the ICO within three months and update within three months of change (reg 14); UK representative for non-UK DSPs (reg 14A); UK nominated person for non-UK OES, changes within 7 days (reg 8A) | RDSPs supply updated registration details within three months of commencement — a statutory "proper address" plus the names of directors or partners; RMSPs register within three months and appoint a UK representative if overseas; data centres and load controllers notify within three months; failure to register becomes an enforceable breach |
| Regulator powers | Information notices with a duty to comply (reg 15); inspections by the authority, an appointee or a directed approved third party (reg 16); enforcement notices preceded by an opportunity to make representations (reg 17); fees for reasonable costs, payable within 30 days (reg 21) | Full cost recovery through published charging schemes after consultation, with end-of-cycle statements; broader information-sharing gateways including law enforcement and non-UK regulators; regulators report annually to the NCSC on incidents; Secretary of State may direct regulators (CAA, Ofcom, Ofgem, DWQR, ICO) and regulated entities on national security grounds |
| Governance and strategy | Statutory post-implementation reviews (9 May 2020, 9 May 2022, then at intervals not exceeding five years) (reg 25) | Statement of strategic priorities that regulators must have regard to and seek to achieve; designated only after a 40-day period in which either House may resolve not to approve it, with an annual report to Parliament on how regulators complied; further reporting duty on NIS legislation from Day 1 |

**CAF as the assessment yardstick.** Competent authorities express "appropriate and proportionate" through the NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework: four objectives (A managing security risk, B protecting against cyber attack, C detecting cyber security events, D minimising impact), 14 principles, and 41 contributing outcomes, each assessed as achieved / partially achieved / not achieved against indicators of good practice (IGPs); a CAF Profile (Basic for all sectors, sector-specific Enhanced) sets the target. CAF v4.0 (released 4 August 2025) added A2.b Understanding Threat and A4.b Secure Software Development and Support, updated C1/C2 monitoring and threat hunting, and threaded AI-related risk through the framework; earlier versions: v1.0 April 2018, v2.0 October 2018, v3.0 September 2019, v3.1 April 2022, v3.2 April 2024. The NCSC has no regulatory role — confirm with the competent authority which CAF version and profile it assesses against. The April 2025 policy statement signals that CAF-style principles will be put on a firmer statutory footing for OES as well as digital services.

## Enforcement and penalties

- **Process today (regs 17–19A).** The regulator informs the entity of the alleged failure and invites representations, may serve an enforcement notice specifying remedial steps and deadlines, and may serve a notice of intention to impose a penalty followed by a penalty notice; penalties and enforcement notices can run in parallel. Before acting the regulator must consider proportionality, representations, remediation steps, time available to comply and overlapping enforcement (reg 23). Appeals lie to the First-tier Tribunal on error of fact, procedural breach, error of law or irrationality (reg 19A). Penalties go to the Consolidated Fund, or the Welsh or Scottish Consolidated Fund where the Welsh Ministers, Scottish Ministers or the Drinking Water Quality Regulator for Scotland imposed them (reg 22).
- **Caps today (reg 18(6)).** Up to £1,000,000 for a non-material contravention; £8,500,000 for a material contravention (a failure of the security, incident-notification or dependency-reporting duties in reg 17(1)(a)–(d) or 17(2)(a)–(d), whether or not an enforcement notice was served — reg 18(7)); £17,000,000 where the material contravention has or could have created a significant risk to or impact on service provision. The government's factsheet says the £17m cap is below what the same regulators can levy elsewhere and has been an insufficient deterrent.
- **Bill (NIS regime).** Two bands fixed in law by duty type: higher band (security and incident-notification duties) the greater of £17m and 4% of worldwide turnover; standard band (e.g., registration failures) the greater of £10m and 2% — flat £17m and £10m where the person is not an undertaking; regulators must weigh mitigation, compliance history and may weigh sector, user impact and growth effects; "turnover" and group attribution to be defined in secondary legislation; daily penalties for continuing breaches are contemplated for data centres; appeals remain with the First-tier Tribunal.
- **Bill (Part 4 national security directions).** Non-compliance with a Secretary of State direction: up to 10% of turnover or £17m (whichever is higher) for undertakings, £17m otherwise, plus up to £100,000 per day for continuing contraventions; information and non-disclosure requirements: up to £10m or £50,000 per day. No criminal offences may be created under the delegated powers.

## Key obligations for security/GRC teams

1. **Determine scope on both timelines.** Test today's status against Schedule 2 thresholds and the RDSP definition, then pre-assess the Bill's data centre (1 MW / 10 MW), RMSP, load controller (300 MW) and critical-supplier tests; record the analysis and its trigger dates. See [../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md).
2. **Keep registrations current.** OES notifications, RDSP registration, UK representatives and nominated persons have three-month clocks (seven days for changes to an OES nominee); plan for re-registration within three months of commencement of the amended regime.
3. **Build the incident playbook around a 24-hour initial notification** even though the law today says 72 hours: pre-agree awareness triggers, the minimal initial content, the 72-hour full-report fields, the NCSC channel and customer-notification decision, and log every clock in [../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md](../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md). See [../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md](../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md) and [../../workflows/incident-regulatory-response.md](../../workflows/incident-regulatory-response.md).
4. **Extend reportability triage beyond outages.** The Bill captures compromises with significant *likely* impact (ransomware pre-positioning, credential compromise of relied-on systems); align severity criteria with the factors listed (users, duration, area, data compromise, downstream systems, economic/societal impact) and with the ICO's RDSP thresholds.
5. **Assess against the CAF and evidence it.** Map controls to the 41 contributing outcomes and the profile your regulator sets; treat the v4.0 additions (threat understanding, secure software development, monitoring and threat hunting, AI risk) as priority gaps. See [../../skills/framework-gap-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/framework-gap-assessment/SKILL.md), [../../skills/control-mapping/SKILL.md](../../skills/control-mapping/SKILL.md) and [../../skills/control-testing/SKILL.md](../../skills/control-testing/SKILL.md).
6. **Manage supply chain as a regulated risk.** Inventory suppliers whose systems the essential or digital service relies on (reg 12(9) already requires dependency reporting); anticipate critical-supplier designation of key vendors and clarified supply-chain duties, and push incident-notification and cooperation clauses into contracts. See [../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md) and [../../workflows/vendor-onboarding.md](../../workflows/vendor-onboarding.md).
7. **Prepare for inspections and information notices.** Regulators can appoint or direct third-party inspectors and require security policies and inspection evidence; failure to comply is a penalisable duty. See [../../skills/audit-preparation/SKILL.md](../../skills/audit-preparation/SKILL.md).
8. **Track the Bill and its secondary legislation** — thresholds, "turnover", security requirements, code of practice, charging schemes and commencement dates all arrive by regulations after consultation. See [../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md) and [../../workflows/new-regulation-impact-assessment.md](../../workflows/new-regulation-impact-assessment.md).
9. **Report to the board** on scope status, CAF outcome coverage, open enforcement/information notices, incident-clock performance and the exposure created by turnover-linked penalties. See [../../skills/grc-metrics-reporting/SKILL.md](../../skills/grc-metrics-reporting/SKILL.md) and [../../templates/grc-board-report.md](../../templates/grc-board-report.md).

## Interplay

- **EU NIS2** ([nis2.md](nis2.md)): the UK is outside NIS2; groups with EU establishments face NIS2 through member-state law in parallel. The Bill borrows NIS2's 24-hour early warning / 72-hour notification structure (the policy statement says the UK process is intended to be "no more onerous") and its MSP and critical-supplier logic, but leaves more detail to secondary legislation and keeps the UK's federated, sector-regulator model and CAF-based supervision. Run one incident taxonomy that satisfies both. See [../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md](../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md).
- **UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018** ([gdpr.md](gdpr.md), [other-jurisdictions.md](other-jurisdictions.md)): a NIS incident involving personal data triggers a separate 72-hour personal-data-breach notification to the ICO regardless of NIS thresholds; for RDSPs and RMSPs the same regulator receives both, but under different powers and content rules.
- **Telecommunications (Security) Act 2021**: providers under Communications Act ss.105A–105C are outside NIS (reg 8(1A)); the Bill's RMSP definition likewise excludes public electronic communications networks and services.
- **Financial services**: DORA ([dora.md](dora.md)) governs EU financial entities; UK firms are under FCA/PRA operational resilience rules and the FSMA 2023 critical third parties regime, which the government cites as the model for critical-supplier designation. A UK MSP serving banks may be an RMSP under NIS and a critical third party under FSMA.
- **EU CER Directive and EU Cyber Resilience Act**: physical/operational resilience of critical entities and product security are separate EU regimes; the Bill's "all-hazards" framing for data centres (heat, power, equipment failure) overlaps CER themes for UK-EU groups.
- **Frameworks**: the CAF is outcome-based and maps naturally to [../frameworks/nist-csf-2.md](../frameworks/nist-csf-2.md) functions and to [../frameworks/iso-27001-2022.md](../frameworks/iso-27001-2022.md) controls; ISO 27001 certification is evidence, not a substitute for a CAF assessment against the regulator's profile. See [../crosswalks/framework-crosswalk.md](../crosswalks/framework-crosswalk.md).
