## Switzerland

**Revised Federal Act on Data Protection (revFADP)**, in force 1 September 2023, aligned Swiss law toward GDPR to preserve EU adequacy (Switzerland holds it — verify status). Applies to processing affecting individuals in Switzerland, with extraterritorial effect. Key features: processing principles and records of processing (small-business exemption), DPIAs for high-risk processing, privacy-by-design/default, cross-border transfers via adequacy list or safeguards (Swiss-adapted SCCs), and **breach notification to the FDPIC "as rapidly as possible"** where the breach likely leads to a high risk to the data subject (individuals notified where required for their protection). Distinctive: penalties are **criminal fines up to CHF 250,000 imposed on responsible individuals**, not corporate administrative fines — personal exposure changes internal accountability conversations. Regulator: Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC).

## Saudi Arabia

**Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL)** (Royal Decree M/19 of 2021, amended 2023; enforcement following a grace period ending September 2024 — verify current posture). Regulator: **SDAIA** (Saudi Data & AI Authority). GDPR-family structure: legal bases (with consent prominent), data subject rights, RoPA, DPO in defined cases, and impact assessments. **Breach notification to SDAIA within 72 hours** of awareness per the implementing regulations, and to data subjects where the breach threatens their data or interests — verify thresholds. Cross-border transfers were liberalized by the 2023 amendment and transfer regulations (adequacy, safeguards including SCCs/BCR-style options, and defined exceptions) — verify current mechanisms. Penalties include fines up to **SAR 5M** per violation (doubling for repeats) and criminal sanctions for disclosure of sensitive data with intent to harm. Data-sovereignty expectations remain strong in regulated sectors (banking via SAMA, health) — check sector rules.

## United Arab Emirates

Three regimes coexist. **Federal PDPL** (Decree-Law 45/2021): GDPR-style skeleton (consent-centric bases, rights, breach notification to the UAE Data Office, cross-border rules) — but its **executive regulations remained pending for years; obligations are largely dormant until they issue — verify current status** before advising. **DIFC Data Protection Law 2020** (Dubai financial free zone): actively enforced GDPR-grade regime with its own Commissioner, adequacy/SCC transfer mechanics, and **72-hour breach notification** where the breach compromises confidentiality/security with risk to data subjects — verify formulation. **ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021** (Abu Dhabi free zone): similar model with its own commissioner and notification duties. Companies in the free zones should treat DIFC/ADGM law as the operative bar; onshore, sector rules (Central Bank, TDRA, health data laws including federal health-data localization) do the near-term work.

## South Africa

**Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA)**, fully effective July 2021. Regulator: **Information Regulator**. Applies to processing by responsible parties domiciled in South Africa (or using means there). Eight processing conditions (accountability through data subject participation), operator (processor) contracts, prior authorization for certain processing, information officer registration with the Regulator, and cross-border transfer conditions (adequate protection, consent, contract necessity). **Security compromises:** notify the Regulator and affected data subjects **"as soon as reasonably possible"** after discovery (delay only for legitimate law-enforcement or investigative needs). Sanctions: enforcement notices, administrative fines up to **R10 million**, and criminal liability (imprisonment) for defined offences. Direct-marketing consent rules (opt-in for electronic marketing) are actively enforced.

## New Zealand

**Privacy Act 2020**. Regulator: **Office of the Privacy Commissioner**. Thirteen Information Privacy Principles; IPP 5 requires reasonable security safeguards. **Notifiable privacy breaches** — those causing or likely to cause **serious harm** — must be reported to the Commissioner and affected individuals **as soon as practicable**; factors for the serious-harm assessment are statutory. Cross-border disclosure (IPP 12) requires comparable safeguards or consent. Penalties are modest (offences up to NZ$10,000; compliance notices and Human Rights Review Tribunal damages are the practical levers). New Zealand holds EU adequacy (verify status). Practical note: the low penalty ceiling makes NZ obligations easy to under-prioritize in global programs — the reputational and tribunal-damages exposure is the real risk.

## Israel

**Protection of Privacy Law (PPL) 1981** plus the **Protection of Privacy Regulations (Data Security) 2017** — a prescriptive, tiered security regime (database classification into security levels with concrete control requirements: access management, audit logging, penetration testing cadence for higher tiers). **Amendment 13** (passed 2024, in force from August 2025 — verify): substantially modernizes the law — expanded definitions aligned toward GDPR concepts, a strengthened **Privacy Protection Authority (PPA)** with significant administrative fining powers, and privacy-officer requirements for defined organizations. Breach handling: the Security Regulations require documentation of incidents and **reporting of severe incidents to the PPA** (with the PPA able to order subject notification) — verify current windows and thresholds under the amended regime. Israel holds EU adequacy (reaffirmed subject to conditions — verify).

## Key obligations for security/GRC teams

1. Determine applicability per jurisdiction by data subjects served, establishment, sector, and infrastructure designation — not by headquarters location (see [../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md)).
2. Build a single breach-clock matrix covering every applicable regime; the binding constraint is usually the shortest sectoral clock (MAS 1 hour, CERT-In 6 hours, SOCI 12 hours), not the privacy law (see [../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md](../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md) and [../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md](../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md)).
3. Map data flows for localization and transfer-mechanism obligations (China first, then Quebec/India/Brazil/Saudi transfer rules) before architecting cross-border systems.
4. Track evolving instruments explicitly: UK Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, Canadian federal privacy reform, India DPDP Rules phase-in, UAE executive regulations, Israel Amendment 13 secondary rules, China incident-reporting measures (see [../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md)).
5. For financial-sector entities, treat APRA CPS 234, MAS TRM, RBI, SAMA, and local equivalents as the operative control baselines — they are examined, prescriptive, and carry the shortest reporting windows.
6. Localize IR playbooks: regulator contact points, report templates, and language requirements per jurisdiction, rehearsed in tabletops.
