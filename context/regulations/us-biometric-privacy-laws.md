# US biometric privacy laws — Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, Washington RCW 19.375 and the state privacy-law overlay

## At a glance

## What it is

US biometric privacy is a patchwork of state statutes, one state privacy-law amendment, a handful of city ordinances and FTC unfair-practice enforcement — there is no federal biometric law. The template is Illinois BIPA (2008): a private entity may not collect a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry without written notice of purpose and retention period and a written release; may not profit from it; may only disclose it in narrow cases; must protect it at least as well as other confidential data; and must publish a retention schedule that destroys it within three years of the individual's last interaction. Texas (2009) and Washington (2017) adopted similar substantive rules but reserved enforcement to their Attorneys General; Colorado (effective 2025) bolted BIPA-style duties onto its comprehensive privacy law.

What makes BIPA singular is its remedy: a private right of action with liquidated damages per violation and no need to show harm beyond the statutory violation (*Rosenbach v. Six Flags*, 2019 IL 123186). Combined with the Illinois Supreme Court's 2023 holding that a claim accrued on every scan or transmission (*Cothron v. White Castle*, 2023 IL 128004 — the defendant estimated class-wide exposure for roughly 9,500 employees at more than $17 billion), BIPA produced the largest privacy class-action docket in the country. The 2024 amendment capped recovery at one per person per collection method, and the Seventh Circuit applied that cap retroactively in April 2026, but timekeeping, facial-recognition, voice-analytics and photo-tagging cases continue to be filed.

The Texas Attorney General has shown that public enforcement of a no-private-action statute can be as costly as class litigation: the first-ever CUBI suit (against Meta's photo tag-suggestion feature) settled for $1.4 billion over five years, and a CUBI/geolocation/incognito suit against Google settled in principle for $1.375 billion.

## Who it covers / Scope

| Regime | Covered actor | Biometric definition | Trigger | Key exclusions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BIPA 740 ILCS 14 | Any "private entity" — individual, partnership, corporation, LLC, association or other group; not state/local agencies or Illinois courts (§10) | "Biometric identifier": retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, scan of hand or face geometry. "Biometric information": anything based on an identifier used to identify a person. Excludes photographs, writing samples, physical descriptions, biological samples for testing, and data captured in a health-care setting or for HIPAA treatment/payment/operations (§10) | Possession, collection, capture, purchase, or receipt through trade of an Illinois person's identifier — no commercial-purpose limiter | GLBA Title V financial institutions and affiliates (§25(c)); no conflict with HIPAA or the X-Ray Retention Act (§25(b)); contractors working for state/local government (§25(e)) |
| CUBI §503.001 | Any "person" | Retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or record of hand or face geometry (§503.001(a)) | Capture "for a commercial purpose" (not defined in §503.001) | Voiceprints retained by GLBA financial institutions (§(e)(1)); since 1 January 2026, biometric identifiers used to develop, train or offer AI models unless the system uniquely identifies a person, and AI used for security/fraud/incident purposes (§(e)(2)–(3)) |
| RCW 19.375 | Any "person" other than a government agency | Data generated by automatic measurements of biological characteristics (fingerprint, voiceprint, retina, iris, other unique patterns) used to identify a specific individual; excludes photos, video/audio recordings and data derived from them, and HIPAA data (§.010(1)) | "Enrolling" an identifier (capturing, converting to a non-reconstructible template, storing in a matching database) for a "commercial purpose" — defined narrowly as sale/disclosure to third parties for marketing unrelated goods; security and law-enforcement purposes are not commercial (§.010(4)–(5)) | GLBA Title V financial institutions; HIPAA-covered activities; law-enforcement authority (§.040) |
| Colorado C.R.S. §6-1-1314 | Any controller that conducts business in Colorado or targets Colorado residents and processes any amount of biometric identifiers — the CPA's 100,000/25,000-consumer thresholds do not apply to the biometric duties (§6-1-1304(1)(b)) | "Biometric identifier": data from technological processing of biological, physical or behavioral characteristics that can uniquely identify a person — fingerprint, voiceprint, retina/iris, facial map/geometry/template, other unique patterns (§6-1-1303(2.4)). Photos, audio and derived data are excluded unless used for identification (§6-1-1303(2.2)) | Collection or processing of a consumer's (including an employee's) biometric identifier | CPA exemptions continue to apply to non-biometric processing; a controller below the general thresholds complies only for its biometric data |
| Portland City Code ch. 34.10 | Private entities operating places of public accommodation in Portland | "Face recognition technologies" — automated one-to-many face matching or characterization | Any use in a place of public accommodation | Compliance with law; user verification to access one's own devices; automatic face detection in social-media apps (§34.10.040) |

Employee data is squarely in scope of the Illinois, Texas and Colorado regimes (Washington's marketing-based "commercial purpose" test will often leave workforce systems outside RCW 19.375): BIPA's "written release" expressly includes a release executed as a condition of employment; CUBI presumes an employer's security-purpose collection expires on termination (§(c-2)); Colorado limits what employers may make a condition of employment (§6-1-1314(6)). Timekeeping and access-control fingerprint/face systems have been the single largest BIPA case category.

## Core obligations

| Obligation | BIPA (740 ILCS 14/15) | CUBI (§503.001) | RCW 19.375.020 | Colorado §6-1-1314 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Notice before collection | In writing: that an identifier is being collected/stored, the specific purpose, and the length of term (§15(b)(1)–(2)) | Inform the individual before capture (§(b)(1)) | Notice through a procedure reasonably designed to be readily available; exact form is context-dependent (§(2)) | Clear, accessible notice that an identifier is collected, its purpose, retention length, and any disclosure to processors (§(4)(a)) |
| Consent | Written release — informed written consent, electronic signature, or employment release (§15(b)(3); §10 as amended 2024) | Consent to capture (§(b)(2)); a publicly available image is not consent unless the individual published it (§(b-1), from 2026) | Notice, consent, or a mechanism to prevent later commercial use (§(1)) | CPA-standard consent; employers may require consent only for secure access, timekeeping, safety/security, and emergencies; no retaliation for refusing other uses (§(6)) |
| Sale / profit | No sale, lease, trade or other profit (§15(c)) | No sale, lease or disclosure except consent for identification after disappearance/death, completing an authorized financial transaction, statutory requirement, or law-enforcement warrant (§(c)(1)) | No sale, lease or disclosure for a commercial purpose without consent, subject to listed exceptions incl. contractual non-redisclosure (§(3)) | No sale, lease or trade (§(4)(b)(I)) |
| Disclosure | Only with consent, to complete a requested financial transaction, as required by law, or under warrant/subpoena (§15(d)) | As above | As above; no use materially inconsistent with the original terms without fresh consent (§(5)) | Only with consent, to complete a financial transaction the consumer requested, to a processor for the purpose consented to, or where required by law (§(4)(b)(II)) |
| Security | Reasonable standard of care within the industry; at least as protective as other confidential and sensitive information (§15(e)) | Reasonable care; same as or more protective than other confidential information (§(c)(2)) | Reasonable care against unauthorized access and acquisition (§(4)(a)) | CPA duty of care plus an incident-response protocol for biometric compromise, including consumer notification under §6-1-716; processors must have a protocol to notify the controller (§(2)(a)(II), §(3)) |
| Retention / destruction | Public written policy with retention schedule and destruction guidelines; destroy when purpose satisfied or within 3 years of last interaction, whichever first; policy must be followed absent warrant/subpoena (§15(a)) | Destroy within a reasonable time and no later than 1 year after the collection purpose expires; longer only where another law requires the underlying document be kept (§(c)(3), (c-1)) | Retain no longer than reasonably necessary for legal compliance, fraud/security protection, or the enrolled service (§(4)(b)) | Written policy with retention schedule and deletion at the earliest of purpose satisfied, 24 months after last interaction, or 45 days (extendable by 45) after an at-least-annual review finds storage unnecessary; policy public except employee-only policies and the internal incident protocol (§(2)) |
| Security-purpose carve-out | None — loss-prevention and fraud uses need the same notice/consent | AI models used for security, fraud and incident response are exempt from 2026 (§(e)(3)) | Notice/consent not required to capture or enroll for a security purpose (§(7)) | Employer safety/security is a permitted condition-of-employment purpose |

Accrual and recovery under BIPA §20 as amended (effective 2 August 2024): repeated collection of the same identifier from the same person by the same method is a **single violation** with at most one recovery (§20(b)); repeated disclosure of the same identifier to the same recipient is likewise a single violation (§20(c)). The amendment reversed the practical effect of *Cothron* and, per the Seventh Circuit (*Clay v. Union Pacific*, *Willis v. Universal Intermodal*, *Gregg v. Central Transport*, Nos. 25-2185/25-2761/25-2762, decided 1 April 2026), applies retroactively to cases pending when enacted because it affects only the remedy.

## Key obligations for security/GRC teams

1. **Inventory every biometric touchpoint** — timekeeping clocks, badge/door access, device unlock managed by the enterprise, contact-center voice authentication, retail loss-prevention cameras, photo tagging, identity-proofing vendors — and map each to the residency of the data subjects (Illinois exposure is the litigation driver). See [../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md).
2. **Publish the retention and destruction policy** with a schedule that satisfies the strictest applicable clock (Colorado 24 months / BIPA 3 years / CUBI 1 year after purpose) and evidence that deletion actually runs — the policy must be followed absent a warrant. Template: [../../templates/policy-template.md](../../templates/policy-template.md); skill: [../../skills/policy-authoring/SKILL.md](../../skills/policy-authoring/SKILL.md).
3. **Capture written, purpose-specific consent before the first scan**, stating purpose and retention term; keep the signed release (electronic signatures are valid under BIPA since August 2024) as a retrievable record per enrollee — missing releases are what plaintiffs subpoena first.
4. **Treat vendors as co-defendants**: timekeeping, HRIS and facial-recognition suppliers have been sued under BIPA in their own right and are the usual disclosure recipients under §15(d). Contract for consent flow-down, retention limits, deletion certificates, no onward sale, and breach notification; Colorado requires processors to have a biometric incident protocol. See [../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md) and [../../templates/vendor-security-questionnaire.md](../../templates/vendor-security-questionnaire.md).
5. **Run a privacy/risk assessment for each biometric use case** — necessity, accuracy and bias (the FTC's unfairness factors), alternatives, and whether a security-purpose carve-out (Washington, Texas 2026) actually applies. Facial recognition in customer-facing settings carries FTC and municipal-ban risk regardless of consent. See [../../skills/dpia-privacy-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/dpia-privacy-assessment/SKILL.md) and [../../templates/dpia-template.md](../../templates/dpia-template.md).
6. **Apply security at least equal to your most sensitive data class**: encryption of templates at rest and in transit, template-only storage (no raw images where avoidable), segregated key management, access logging, and destruction verification. Test these as controls, not policies — see [../../skills/control-testing/SKILL.md](../../skills/control-testing/SKILL.md).
7. **Wire biometric compromise into incident response**: Colorado requires a documented protocol and consumer notice; many state breach statutes now list biometric data as personal information. See [../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md](../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md) and [../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md](../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md).
8. **Gate AI training and analytics on faces/voices** through AI governance: Texas exempts AI model development unless the system uniquely identifies individuals and re-imposes penalties if training data is later repurposed; BIPA has no such carve-out. See [../../skills/ai-governance/SKILL.md](../../skills/ai-governance/SKILL.md) and [../../workflows/ai-system-intake.md](../../workflows/ai-system-intake.md).
9. **Record gaps as formal exceptions with litigation-exposure sizing** (enrollees × statutory damages) rather than as open audit findings — see [../../skills/exception-management/SKILL.md](../../skills/exception-management/SKILL.md) and [../../skills/risk-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/risk-assessment/SKILL.md).

## Interplay

- **Comprehensive state privacy laws** ([us-state-privacy.md](us-state-privacy.md)): biometric data processed to identify a person is "sensitive data" under the state comprehensive laws, and most require opt-in consent (California instead gives a right to limit use) plus data-protection assessments. Those laws exempt small controllers by volume; Colorado's biometric section deliberately does not. Washington's My Health My Data Act (private right of action) may separately reach biometric data tied to health status (verify).
- **HIPAA** ([hipaa.md](hipaa.md)): BIPA §10 and RCW 19.375.010 exclude data collected for HIPAA treatment, payment or operations (CUBI §503.001 has no HIPAA carve-out; Colorado relies on the CPA's general exemptions) — but a hospital's employee timeclock or visitor face-matching system is not HIPAA data and remains in scope everywhere.
- **GLBA** ([glba-ftc-safeguards.md](glba-ftc-safeguards.md)): BIPA §25(c) and RCW 19.375.040 exclude GLBA Title V financial institutions entirely; CUBI exempts only their voiceprints. Fintech and non-bank service providers should not assume the exemption travels with the bank's data.
- **GDPR** ([gdpr.md](gdpr.md)): biometric data for unique identification is an Art. 9 special category (explicit consent or another Art. 9(2) ground, DPIA under Art. 35). A single global consent and retention design can satisfy both if it meets BIPA's written-release and 3-year ceiling; GDPR's storage-limitation principle does not itself fix a clock.
- **EU AI Act** ([eu-ai-act.md](eu-ai-act.md)): remote biometric identification and emotion recognition are prohibited or high-risk practices; Portland's ordinance and the FTC's Rite Aid order are the closest US analogues to those use-based restrictions.
- **Breach law**: biometric data is listed personal information in many state breach statutes and Colorado ties biometric incident protocols to its breach statute (§6-1-716) — see [../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md](../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md).
- **Control frameworks**: map consent capture, retention enforcement and template protection to data-governance and protection outcomes in [../frameworks/nist-csf-2.md](../frameworks/nist-csf-2.md) so biometric evidence is produced by the same controls as other sensitive-data classes.
