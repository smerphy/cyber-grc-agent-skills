# Latin American data protection regimes (Mexico, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Uruguay — plus Costa Rica and Ecuador)

Comparative pack for the Spanish-speaking Latin American privacy laws a multinational GRC program most often meets. Brazil's LGPD is covered separately in [brazil-lgpd.md](brazil-lgpd.md). Every regime below is a general, cross-sector law with a single national authority; the practical differences are breach clocks, registration duties, and whether GDPR-style accountability tools (DPO, DPIA) are mandatory.

## What it is

The region's first-generation laws (Argentina 2000, Uruguay 2008, Mexico 2010, Costa Rica 2011, Peru 2011, Colombia 2012) were modelled on the EU 1995 Directive: consent-centred, database-registration heavy, with modest fines. A second generation is now layering GDPR-style accountability on top: Uruguay's 2018–2020 reforms (extraterritorial scope, 72-hour breach notice, DPO, DPIA), Ecuador's 2021 LOPDP (GDPR-style extraterritorial scope, turnover-based fines, mandatory DPO and DPIA), Peru's 2024 regulation (48-hour breach notice, DPO, digital-environment incident registry), Mexico's 2025 replacement statute (new regulator, UMA-denominated fines), and Chile's Ley 21.719, which rewrites Ley 19.628 wholesale and creates an independent agency with revenue-based fines.

Two structural features matter for program design. First, several regimes still carry **registration** obligations (Argentina, Colombia, Peru, Uruguay, Costa Rica, Ecuador) that GDPR-trained teams tend to overlook, and Colombia's breach-reporting duty is delivered *through* the registry. Second, breach clocks range from 48 hours to 15 business days with different triggers, so the region cannot be run on a single GDPR playbook — see [../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md](../crosswalks/breach-notification-timelines.md).

### Country notes

- **Mexico.** The 20 March 2025 decree issued three laws at once (transparency, public-sector data, private-sector data), abrogated the 5 July 2010 LFPDPPP and the 2017 LGPDPPSO, and transferred INAI's staff, files and pending proceedings to the Secretaría Anticorrupción y Buen Gobierno. Rights of access, rectification, cancellation and opposition (ARCO) must be answered within 20 days (Art. 31). The Chamber of Deputies still publishes the 21 December 2011 Reglamento as "texto vigente" as of September 2026, although Transitory Twelfth gave the Executive 90 calendar days from entry into force to harmonise regulations; no replacement regulation has issued. The 14 November 2025 reform is a procedural harmonisation (Art. 4 now refers to the Código Nacional de Procedimientos Civiles y Familiares).
- **Argentina.** The law predates breach notification and DPOs entirely; AAIP Resolución 47/2018 replaced the 2006/2008 security dispositions with *recommended* controls, and the AAIP describes the need to update Ley 25.326 — a replacement bill's parliamentary status should be checked before relying on it (verify). Fines are still denominated in the nominal peso amounts of the 2000 text (Art. 31).
- **Chile.** Ley 21.719 was promulgated 25 November 2024 and published 13 December 2024; its amendments to Ley 19.628 apply from the first day of the 24th month after publication (1 December 2026). Transitory Second required implementing regulations within six months of publication (the Art. 26 regulation within six months of entry into force); Transitory Fourth set the first Consejo Directivo appointment six months before entry into force, and until commencement the Consejo may only exercise the functions in Art. 30 bis (a), (b), (g), (h) and Art. 30 ter. Transitory Third requires the Servicio de Registro Civil e Identificación to delete the old Art. 22 database registry in the sixty days before commencement. Omitting breach communications or records is a *grave* infraction (Art. 34 ter (k)); deliberately omitting them is *gravísima* (Art. 34 quáter (f)); missing a required DPIA is also gravísima.
- **Colombia.** The consolidated statute (updated 31 August 2026 on the Senate database) shows no amendment to the sanction, transfer or breach provisions. Complaints must be resolved within 15 business days (+8 with notice) before a data subject may escalate to the SIC (Arts. 15–16). Incident reports in the RNBD are not publicly visible.
- **Peru.** DS 016-2024-JUS (published 30 November 2024) entered into force 120 calendar days later and repealed DS 003-2013-JUS. Private-sector DPO obligations phase in by annual sales: >2,300 UIT one year after publication (Nov 2025); 1,700–2,300 UIT two years; 150–1,700 UIT three years; ≤150 UIT four years. DPIAs remain voluntary under Reglamento Art. 40.1. The ANPD published a run of first-instance sanction resolutions against banks, payment and consumer-finance firms and public bodies between March and July 2026.
- **Uruguay.** Ley 19.670 added extraterritorial scope, breach notice and DPOs; Decreto 64/020 (17 Feb 2020) fixed the 72-hour clock, DPIA triggers and the 35,000-person DPO threshold. Registration of databases and codes of conduct remains active (URCDP resolutions through September 2026).
- **Costa Rica.** Ley 8968 (La Gaceta 170, 5 Sept 2011) requires express consent for transfers (Art. 14), registration of databases administered for distribution, dissemination or commercialisation (Art. 21), and optional PRODHAB-registered "protocolos de actuación" (Art. 12), which raise an *iuris tantum* presumption of compliance. The statute itself sets no breach-notification clock; any deadline sits in the implementing regulation, which could not be retrieved from an official source for this pack (verify).
- **Ecuador.** The LOPDP (Registro Oficial, Quinto Suplemento 459, 26 May 2021) tracks the GDPR closely: extraterritorial scope (Art. 3), mandatory DPIA for profiling with legal effects, large-scale special categories and large-scale public-area monitoring (Art. 42), mandatory DPO for the public sector, permanent systematic monitoring and large-scale special-category processing (Art. 48), and a Registro Nacional reporting duty (Art. 51). Breaches go to the data-protection authority and to ARCOTEL as soon as possible and at the latest within five días de término (Art. 43; processors notify controllers within two), and to affected data subjects within three días de término where fundamental rights are at risk (Art. 46). Corrective measures and sanctions have applied since 26 May 2023 (Transitory First).

## Key obligations for security/GRC teams

1. **Map entities and processing to each regime's applicability test** — Chile and Uruguay reach foreign controllers that target or monitor residents; Colombia and Peru key off in-country processing and treaties. See [../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-applicability/SKILL.md).
2. **Build a per-country breach matrix**: Peru 48 h, Uruguay 72 h (mitigation from 24 h), Ecuador 5 días de término to the authority and ARCOTEL (3 to subjects), Colombia 15 business days via the RNBD, Chile without undue delay, Mexico immediate subject notice. Log each clock in [../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md](../../templates/incident-regulatory-notification-log.md) and run [../../workflows/incident-regulatory-response.md](../../workflows/incident-regulatory-response.md); see [../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md](../../skills/incident-regulatory-reporting/SKILL.md).
3. **Register or report databases where required** (Argentina, Colombia above 100,000 UVT, Peru, Uruguay, Ecuador, Costa Rica commercial databases) and calendar Colombia's semi-annual claims updates; unregistered processing is itself sanctionable in Costa Rica.
4. **Appoint DPOs against the thresholds** — Peru's sales-based phase-in, Uruguay's 35,000-person test and Ecuador's Art. 48 triggers — and notify the authority within 15 (Peru) or 90 (Uruguay) days.
5. **Run DPIAs** for the mandatory triggers — Chile Art. 15 ter, Uruguay Decreto Art. 6, Ecuador Art. 42 (Peru's Art. 40 DPIA is voluntary but is the ANPD's expected evidence of accountability) — using [../../templates/dpia-template.md](../../templates/dpia-template.md) and [../../skills/dpia-privacy-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/dpia-privacy-assessment/SKILL.md); Uruguay requires reporting significant residual risk to URCDP.
6. **Contract transfers**: adequacy findings are national (SIC, Agencia, URCDP); use transmission/processor contracts (Colombia Decreto 1377 Art. 25), Peru's transferor burden of proof, and Council of Europe clauses in Uruguay. See [../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md](../../skills/third-party-risk-assessment/SKILL.md).
7. **Align the control set** to Chile Art. 14 quinquies and Uruguay Decreto Art. 3 (both cite recognised standards) via [../frameworks/iso-27001-2022.md](../frameworks/iso-27001-2022.md) and [../../skills/control-mapping/SKILL.md](../../skills/control-mapping/SKILL.md); Chile's Agencia will set size-differentiated minimums by general instruction.
8. **Track the moving parts** — Chile's 1 December 2026 commencement, Mexico's post-INAI regulations, Argentina's reform — through [../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md](../../skills/regulatory-horizon-scanning/SKILL.md) and [../../workflows/new-regulation-impact-assessment.md](../../workflows/new-regulation-impact-assessment.md).

## Interplay

- **GDPR.** Argentina and Uruguay adequacy lets EU data flow without SCCs, but the reverse direction still needs each country's own mechanism; Chile and Uruguay copy GDPR's extraterritorial test, and Chile's DPIA triggers and breach-register duty mirror Arts. 33–35. See [gdpr.md](gdpr.md).
- **Brazil LGPD.** Regional groups usually run Brazil as a separate stream — see [brazil-lgpd.md](brazil-lgpd.md) for its ANPD breach clock and revenue-based fine cap; the 26 January 2026 mutual EU–Brazil adequacy decisions change EU–Brazil flows, not intra-regional ones.
- **Sector overlays.** Colombia's Ley 1266/2008 governs credit data outside Ley 1581; Mexico excludes credit-information companies; Peru routes digital incidents to the national digital-security registry in parallel with the ANPD; Ecuador requires parallel notice to the telecoms regulator ARCOTEL; Uruguay's URCDP coordinates breaches with CERTuy.
- **Public sector.** Mexico's LGPDPPSO (breach notice "sin dilación alguna" to the Secretaría, Art. 34) and Chile's Title IV impose distinct duties on public bodies; Colombia refers public-entity breaches to the Procuraduría.
- **US comparison.** Enforcement is administrative, with habeas data actions for individuals; civil damages claims are preserved (Argentina Art. 31, Chile Art. 47) but there is no CCPA-style statutory-damages regime. See [us-state-privacy.md](us-state-privacy.md).
